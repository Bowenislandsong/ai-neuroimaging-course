Course bibliography: key references by lesson

Every computational lesson opens with a short Why this technique matters note and two to four key references for the technique it teaches: at least one foundational or standard-tool paper and at least one published in 2019 or later. They complement the 24-paper library, which sets each lesson's research question, and do not replace it.

Generated by scripts/build_reading_indexes.py from key_references.json. scripts/verify_references.py checked every entry against Crossref, OpenAlex and arXiv on 2026-09-26 (reference_verification.json): title, year and first author must match the DOI or arXiv record. Citation counts are OpenAlex cited_by_count values on that date. They change daily, favor older papers, and indicate reach rather than quality.

Foundations

F01 · What the AI does and what you must understand

Technique: Input-to-output transformation contracts, axis semantics, and temporal scaling invariants (demeaning, percent signal change, z-scoring)

Every neuroimaging preprocessing and modeling step transforms array shapes, physical units, and statistical conditioning sets. Without an explicit transformation contract, a syntactically valid snippet can demean across space instead of time (passing a naive global-mean check while injecting global-signal artifacts) or alter group-level GLM effect sizes (beta_hat) across coil sensitivity profiles even though single-voxel t-statistics remain invariant.

  • Foundational. Nichols TE, Das S, Eickhoff SB, Evans AC, Glatard T, Hanke M, Kriegeskorte N, Milham MP, Poldrack RA, Poline JB, Proal E, Thirion B, Van Essen DC, White T, Yeo BTT (2017). Best practices in data analysis and sharing in neuroimaging using MRI. Nature Neuroscience. Cited by 732 (OpenAlex); MATCH.
    Establishes the COBIDAS standards for explicitly reporting analytical contracts, parameterization, scaling, and software provenance in MRI workflows.

  • Critical evaluation. Botvinik-Nezer R, Holzmeister F, Camerer CF, Dreber A, Huber J, Johannesson M, Kirchler M, Iwanir R, Mumford JA, Adcock RA, Avesani P, Baczkowski BM, Bajracharya A, Bakst L, Ball S (2020). Variability in the analysis of a single neuroimaging dataset by many teams. Nature. Cited by 1,229 (OpenAlex); MATCH. Course library: PD01.
    Demonstrates across 70 teams that unstandardized analytical and scaling contracts yield substantial divergence in binary fMRI hypothesis decisions on identical data.

  • Review. Poldrack RA, Huckins G, Varoquaux G (2020). Establishment of Best Practices for Evidence for Prediction: A Review. JAMA Psychiatry. Cited by 820 (OpenAlex); MATCH.
    Defines rigorous input-output validation contracts, train/test separation rules, and metric selection standards for predictive neuroimaging models.

F02 · From tissue to MRI contrast to BOLD

Technique: Bloch relaxation (T1, T2, T2*), Gradient-Echo BOLD sensitivity, and neurovascular hemodynamic coupling

MRI voxel intensities are sequence-weighted echo amplitudes rather than direct measurements of tissue density or spiking activity. Understanding how TR, TE, and flip angle invert tissue contrast, why BOLD contrast peaks near TE = T2* while suffering susceptibility dropout near air-tissue sinuses, and why BOLD reflects perisynaptic LFP input filtered through a sluggish, region-specific hemodynamic response (Logothetis et al., 2001) prevents reverse-inference and vascular-latency confounds in fMRI.

  • Foundational. Ogawa S, Lee TM, Kay AR, Tank DW (1990). Brain magnetic resonance imaging with contrast dependent on blood oxygenation. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. Cited by 6,589 (OpenAlex); MATCH.
    Introduces Blood-Oxygenation-Level-Dependent (BOLD) MRI contrast arising from paramagnetic deoxyhemoglobin field gradients shortening T2*.

  • Foundational. Buxton RB, Wong EC, Frank LR (1998). Dynamics of blood flow and oxygenation changes during brain activation: The balloon model. Magnetic Resonance in Medicine. Cited by 1,726 (OpenAlex); MATCH.
    Formulates the biomechanical Balloon model linking cerebral blood flow surges, venous volume expansion, and deoxyhemoglobin clearance to the delayed BOLD impulse response.

  • Foundational. Logothetis NK, Pauls J, Augath M, Trinath T, Oeltermann A (2001). Neurophysiological investigation of the basis of the fMRI signal. Nature. Cited by 6,524 (OpenAlex); MATCH. Course library: PB01.
    Simultaneous intracortical electrophysiology and 4.7 T fMRI proving that BOLD correlates more strongly with perisynaptic local field potentials (LFPs) than with multi-unit spiking output (MUA).

  • Review. Drew PJ (2019). Vascular and neural basis of the BOLD signal. Current Opinion in Neurobiology. Cited by 150 (OpenAlex); MATCH.
    Synthesizes modern cellular and vascular mechanisms of neurovascular coupling, highlighting how interneurons, non-neural drives, and regional vascular dynamics shape BOLD signals.

F03 · Read a snippet without becoming a software engineer

Technique: Forensic Python/NumPy code auditing: broadcasting semantics, domain guards, view vs. copy aliasing, mask indexing, and fold-isolated feature selection

The most dangerous bugs in scientific Python are silent semantic errors that execute without raising exceptions—such as missing keepdims=True on square matrices, mutating shared-memory NumPy slice views in place, averaging across unmasked background zeros, or screening features before cross-validation (Kriegeskorte et al., 2009; Rosenblatt et al., 2024). Auditing code line-by-line as a mathematical contract prevents fabricated decoding accuracy and corrupted baselines.

  • Foundational. Kriegeskorte N, Simmons WK, Bellgowan PSF, Baker CI (2009). Circular analysis in systems neuroscience: the dangers of double dipping. Nature Neuroscience. Cited by 2,869 (OpenAlex); MATCH. Course library: PD04.
    Proves how selecting voxels or features using full-dataset statistics prior to evaluation fabricates spurious effects out of noise.

  • Standard tool. Abraham A, Pedregosa F, Eickenberg M, Gervais P, Mueller A, Kossaifi J, Gramfort A, Thirion B, Varoquaux G (2014). Machine learning for neuroimaging with scikit-learn. Frontiers in Neuroinformatics. Cited by 2,732 (OpenAlex); MATCH.
    Introduces the Nilearn masking contract (3D/4D volumes to 2D masked arrays) and fold-isolated scikit-learn Pipeline patterns for neuroimaging.

  • Standard tool. Harris CR, Millman KJ, van der Walt SJ, Gommers R, Virtanen P, Cournapeau D, Wieser E, Taylor J, Berg S, Smith NJ, Kern R, Picus M, Hoyer S, van Kerkwijk MH, Brett M (2020). Array programming with NumPy. Nature. Cited by 23,967 (OpenAlex); MATCH.
    Defines the canonical ndarray memory model, strided view vs. copy semantics, and right-aligned broadcasting rules underlying Python neuroimaging arrays.

  • Critical evaluation. Rosenblatt M, Tejavibulya L, Jiang R, Noble S, Scheinost D (2024). Data leakage inflates prediction performance in connectome-based machine learning models. Nature Communications. Cited by 210 (OpenAlex); MATCH.
    Quantifies how feature-selection, covariate, and subject-level leakage across cross-validation folds severely inflate neuroimaging prediction metrics.

F04 · A notebook is an experiment with state

Technique: Jupyter kernel state hygiene, PRNG stream isolation (SeedSequence), IEEE-754 floating-point determinism, and cryptographic provenance

Interactive notebooks execute in temporal kernel order rather than top-to-bottom visual order, allowing out-of-order cell runs, in-place state mutations, and coupled global random seeds to silently invalidate published outputs (Pimentel et al., 2019). Distinguishing bitwise SHA-256 reproducibility, numerical tolerance reproducibility (under IEEE-754 float32 non-associativity and catastrophic cancellation), and scientific replicability is essential for trustworthy neuroimaging workflows (Glatard et al., 2015).

  • Foundational. Sandve GK, Nekrutenko A, Taylor J, Hovig E (2013). Ten Simple Rules for Reproducible Computational Research. PLoS Computational Biology. Cited by 890 (OpenAlex); MATCH.
    Establishes foundational practices for tracking execution provenance, software versions, random seeds, and automated end-to-end regeneration of results.

  • Critical evaluation. Glatard T, Lewis LB, Ferreira da Silva R, Adalat R, Beck N, Lepage C, Rioux P, Rousseau M, Sherif T, Deelman E, Khalili-Mahani N, Evans AC (2015). Reproducibility of neuroimaging analyses across operating systems. Frontiers in Neuroinformatics. Cited by 154 (OpenAlex); MATCH.
    Demonstrates how small IEEE-754 floating-point and math-library differences across operating systems propagate into divergent FreeSurfer and FSL neuroimaging outputs.

  • Critical evaluation. Pimentel JF, Murta L, Braganholo V, Freire J (2019). A Large-Scale Study About Quality and Reproducibility of Jupyter Notebooks. 2019 IEEE/ACM 16th International Conference on Mining Software Repositories (MSR). Cited by 245 (OpenAlex); MATCH.
    Audits over 1.4 million GitHub Jupyter notebooks, showing that out-of-order execution state and missing dependency specifications cause most notebooks to fail reproduction.

  • Review. Rule A, Birmingham A, Zuniga C, Altintas I, Huang S, Knight R, Moshiri N, Nguyen MH, Rosenthal SB, Pérez F, Rose PW (2019). Ten simple rules for writing and sharing computational analyses in Jupyter Notebooks. PLOS Computational Biology. Cited by 196 (OpenAlex); MATCH.
    Provides actionable guidelines for cell idempotency, clean top-to-bottom kernel execution, and modular function design in scientific notebooks.

Processing

PR01 · Read an image without losing its location

Technique: Voxel-to-world affine geometry, NIfTI qform/sform headers, and BIDS metadata contracts

Every neuroimaging operation—from cross-modal EPI-to-T1w registration and susceptibility distortion unwarping in fMRIPrep to 3D keypoint alignment in BrainMorph—depends on mapping discrete 0-indexed voxel arrays into continuous physical millimeter coordinates (RAS+) and reading pulse-sequence timing from BIDS JSON sidecars. Stripping NIfTI affines (resetting to an identity matrix), flipping array axes without post-multiplying the affine by the corresponding reflection-translation matrix, or ignoring sform/qform shear divergence silently swaps cerebral hemispheres or shifts coordinates by tens of millimeters without altering image intensity histograms. Mastering the 4x4 homogeneous affine decomposition and header precedence rules forces explicit verification of handedness (sign of det(M)), voxel volume (|det(M)|), and BIDS metadata completeness before running any downstream model.

  • Foundational. Gorgolewski KJ, Auer T, Calhoun VD, Craddock RC, Das S, Duff EP, Flandin G, Ghosh SS, Glatard T, Halchenko YO, Handwerker DA, Hanke M, Keator D, Li X, Michael Z, Maumet C, Nichols BN, Nichols TE, Pellman J, Poline JB, Rokem A, Schaefer G, Sochat V, Triplett W, Turner JA, Varoquaux G, Poldrack RA (2016). The brain imaging data structure, a format for organizing and describing outputs of neuroimaging experiments. Scientific Data. Cited by 1,988 (OpenAlex); MATCH.
    Defines the Brain Imaging Data Structure (BIDS) standard that pairs NIfTI volumes with machine-readable JSON metadata sidecars for acquisition timing and phase-encoding polarity.

  • Standard tool. Li X, Morgan PS, Ashburner J, Smith J, Rorden C (2016). The first step for neuroimaging data analysis: DICOM to NIfTI conversion. Journal of Neuroscience Methods. Cited by 1,169 (OpenAlex); MATCH.
    Establishes how dcm2niix converts raw scanner DICOM headers into NIfTI-1 qform/sform affine matrices and BIDS JSON sidecars while handling gantry tilt and slice orientation.

  • State of the art. Esteban O, Markiewicz CJ, Blair RW, Moodie CA, Isik AI, Erramuzpe A, Kent JD, Goncalves M, DuPre E, Snyder M, Oya H, Ghosh SS, Wright J, Durnez J, Poldrack RA, Gorgolewski KJ (2019). fMRIPrep: a robust preprocessing pipeline for functional MRI. Nature Methods. Cited by 4,411 (OpenAlex); MATCH. Course library: PP01.
    Demonstrates how fMRIPrep automates BIDS metadata validation, qform/sform header reconciliation, and canonical RAS+ reorientation at the entry point of preprocessing.

PR02 · Estimate alignment, then choose how to sample

Technique: Affine image registration, similarity cost functions, backward pull resampling, and interpolation transfer functions

Aligning multi-modal and longitudinal brain scans requires decoupling transformation estimation (via intensity cost functions like Mutual Information or closed-form keypoint least squares) from grid resampling. Forward ('push') coordinate rounding creates unvisited grid holes and collisions, interpolating discrete parcellation masks with linear or spline kernels fabricates invalid anatomical label IDs along ROI boundaries, and applying sequential resamplings across pipeline stages compounds low-pass spatial filtering that biases smoothness-dependent statistical inference. Understanding backward ('pull') sampling, kernel frequency responses, and single-shot composed transformations is essential for building artifact-free registration workflows in FSL FLIRT, ANTs, fMRIPrep, and BrainMorph.

  • Foundational. Maes F, Collignon A, Vandermeulen D, Marchal G, Suetens P (1997). Multimodality image registration by maximization of mutual information. IEEE Transactions on Medical Imaging. Cited by 4,560 (OpenAlex); MATCH.
    Introduces mutual information maximization for accurate cross-modality 3D brain image registration without requiring linear intensity relationships.

  • Standard tool. Jenkinson M, Smith S (2001). A global optimisation method for robust affine registration of brain images. Medical Image Analysis. Cited by 7,273 (OpenAlex); MATCH.
    Defines the multi-scale global optimization method underlying FSL FLIRT for robust 6-DOF rigid and 12-DOF affine brain registration.

  • State of the art. Hoffmann M, Billot B, Greve DN, Iglesias JE, Fischl B, Dalca AV (2022). SynthMorph: Learning Contrast-Invariant Registration Without Acquired Images. IEEE Transactions on Medical Imaging. Cited by 205 (OpenAlex); MATCH.
    Demonstrates contrast-invariant deep learning image registration trained entirely on synthetic label-derived images using spatial transformer pull resampling.

PR03 · Read a deformation field and detect folding

Technique: Nonlinear deformable registration, Jacobian determinant topology auditing, spatial regularization, and stationary velocity field integration

Nonlinear registration aligns subject-specific cortical folding and subcortical morphology to a common template using millions of displacement parameters, making the optimization inherently ill-posed without spatial regularization. An unregularized warp can achieve deceptively high intensity similarity or regional Dice overlap while folding tissue over itself (non-positive Jacobian determinant, J(x) <= 0), destroying anatomical topology and invalidating downstream cortical surface projection or morphometry. Computing the deformation gradient tensor F(x) = I + grad u(x), auditing log-Jacobian maps and folding percentages, and integrating stationary velocity fields via scaling-and-squaring (phi = exp(v)) are mandatory to guarantee invertible, diffeomorphic transformations.

  • Foundational. Ashburner J (2007). A fast diffeomorphic image registration algorithm. NeuroImage. Cited by 8,480 (OpenAlex); MATCH.
    Introduces the DARTEL stationary velocity field parameterization and scaling-and-squaring ODE integration for diffeomorphic brain registration.

  • Standard tool. Avants BB, Epstein CL, Grossman M, Gee JC (2008). Symmetric diffeomorphic image registration with cross-correlation: Evaluating automated labeling of elderly and neurodegenerative brain. Medical Image Analysis. Cited by 6,206 (OpenAlex); MATCH.
    Establishes Symmetric Normalization (SyN) in ANTs for large-deformation diffeomorphic registration with cross-correlation.

  • State of the art. Balakrishnan G, Zhao A, Sabuncu MR, Guttag J, Dalca AV (2019). VoxelMorph: A Learning Framework for Deformable Medical Image Registration. IEEE Transactions on Medical Imaging. Cited by 2,175 (OpenAlex); MATCH.
    Formulates unsupervised deep learning deformable registration (VoxelMorph) combining image similarity losses with spatial gradient regularization and Jacobian folding evaluation.

  • Critical evaluation. Klein A, Andersson J, Ardekani BA, Ashburner J, Avants B, Chiang MC, Christensen GE, Collins DL, Gee J, Hellier P, Song JH, Jenkinson M, Lepage C, Rueckert D, Thompson P, Vercauteren T, Woods RP, Mann JJ, Parsey RV (2009). Evaluation of 14 nonlinear deformation algorithms applied to human brain MRI registration. NeuroImage. Cited by 2,339 (OpenAlex); MATCH.
    Benchmarks 14 nonlinear deformation algorithms across labeled human brain MRI datasets, demonstrating the superiority of diffeomorphic methods such as SyN and DARTEL.

PR04 · Separate smooth intensity bias from tissue contrast

Technique: Bias-field (RF intensity non-uniformity) modeling, homomorphic log-filtering, and N4ITK correction

Radiofrequency transmit (B1+) standing-wave interference and phased-array receive (B1-) coil sensitivity drop-off modulate true tissue contrast by a smooth multiplicative gain field B(x) that scales both class means and within-tissue variances (proportionally to mu_c^2). Left uncorrected, low-frequency shading causes white matter in dimly illuminated regions to fall below gray matter intensities in bright peripheral regions, breaking global thresholds, Gaussian mixture models, and registration cost functions. PR07 · Measure volume before making an atrophy claim

PR17 · Separate denoising, Gibbs removal, eddy correction and interpolation

Technique: Diffusion MRI preprocessing: MP-PCA thermal denoising, Gibbs unringing, susceptibility/eddy distortion correction, and single-step composed interpolation

Composing susceptibility, eddy-current, and motion transforms into a single final resampling step with B-table rotation preserves anatomical edge sharpness and prevents directional bias in downstream tensor and tractography models.

PR18 · Fit a tensor and interpret FA and MD within its limits

Technique: Diffusion Tensor Imaging (DTI): 6-parameter B-matrix design, OLS vs. heteroscedastic WLS estimation, spectral invariants (MD, AD, RD, FA), and Westin shape indices

Diffusion Tensor Imaging (Basser et al., 1994) is the most widely deployed microstructural MRI model in clinical and cognitive neuroscience, summarizing directional water diffusivity via scalar invariants such as Fractional Anisotropy (FA) and Mean Diffusivity (MD). Without understanding heteroscedastic log-domain noise (Salvador et al., 2005; Veraart et al., 2013), unweighted OLS fits over-weight noisy high-attenuation directions, and without checking physical MD units, scale-invariant FA masks 1000x b-value unit errors. Crucially, because 60%–90% of white-matter voxels contain crossing fibers (Jeurissen et al., 2013), a single tensor collapses into an oblate ellipsoid with reduced FA, forcing researchers to use Westin shape indices or multi-fiber models rather than misinterpreting lower FA as demyelination (Jones et al., 2013; Figley et al., 2022).

PR19 · A streamline is a model trajectory, not an axon

Research design

D01 · The question is an estimand, not a button

Technique: Causal estimands, potential outcomes, DAG identification, and inverse-probability / standardization adjustment

Every neuroimaging analysis—from multi-team task-fMRI pipelines (NARPS) to biobank brain-wide association studies (UK Biobank, ABCD)—implicitly targets a specific causal or descriptive estimand before any regression or machine-learning estimator is chosen. Without explicitly specifying the unit-level potential outcomes, target population, and confounder set on a causal DAG, researchers conflate analytical flexibility that changes the scientific question (e.g., conditioning on colliders or altering parametric modulators) with mere estimator variance, and risk severe bias when covariate overlap (positivity) fails across sites or clinical groups.

  • Foundational. Rubin DB (1974). Estimating causal effects of treatments in randomized and nonrandomized studies.. Journal of Educational Psychology. Cited by 9,621 (OpenAlex); MATCH.
    Formalizes causal effects as comparisons of potential outcomes Y(1) and Y(0) on a common set of units, establishing why observational comparisons require explicit assignment-mechanism assumptions.

  • Foundational. ROSENBAUM PR, RUBIN DB (1983). The central role of the propensity score in observational studies for causal effects. Biometrika. Cited by 31,600 (OpenAlex); MATCH.
    Introduces the propensity score e(X)=P(A=1|X) and proves that conditioning or weighting on e(X) suffices to remove confounding from observed covariates X under strong ignorability and positivity.

  • Critical evaluation. Lundberg I, Johnson R, Stewart BM (2021). What Is Your Estimand? Defining the Target Quantity Connects Statistical Evidence to Theory. American Sociological Review. Cited by 379 (OpenAlex); MATCH.

  • Review. Hernán MA, Robins JM (2006). Estimating causal effects from epidemiological data. Journal of Epidemiology and Community Health. Cited by 1,063 (OpenAlex); MATCH.
    Clearly links potential-outcome estimands, exchangeability, positivity, and consistency to inverse-probability weighting and standardization in observational studies.

D02 · Sampling, missingness, and the people behind rows

Technique: Hierarchical sampling units, pseudoreplication, missingness mechanisms (MCAR/MAR/MNAR), collider selection bias, and inverse-probability of selection weighting (IPSW)

Large-scale neuroimaging cohorts (ABCD, HCP, UK Biobank) combine multi-run, multi-visit, and family-clustered observations with stringent quality-control exclusions (especially head-motion scrubbing) that systematically remove younger participants and individuals with higher clinical symptom severity. Treating repeated scans or twin pairs as independent rows inflates effective sample size by the Kish design effect and causes high false-positive rates (pseudoreplication), while complete-case analysis after motion-related exclusions conditions on a selection collider or MAR/MNAR mechanism that biases brain-behavior estimates unless hierarchical models and inverse-probability retention weights are used.

  • Foundational. RUBIN DB (1976). Inference and missing data. Biometrika. Cited by 9,872 (OpenAlex); MATCH.
    Defines the MCAR, MAR, and MNAR missing-data taxonomy and gives the exact conditions under which missingness mechanisms can be ignored for likelihood or frequentist inference.

  • Foundational. Hernán MA, Hernández-Díaz S, Robins JM (2004). A Structural Approach to Selection Bias. Epidemiology. Cited by 2,851 (OpenAlex); MATCH.
    Unifies selection bias across study designs as conditioning on a common effect (collider) of exposure and outcome on a causal DAG, such as analyzing only retained low-motion scans.

    Demonstrates how treating repeated measurements, runs, or related observations as independent sample size N inflates degrees of freedom and Type I error across neuroscience.

D03 · Reliability is about differences between people

Technique: Classical test theory, intraclass correlation coefficients ICC(2,1) vs ICC(3,1), Spearman-Brown multi-session aggregation, and Spearman attenuation correction

A neuroimaging measure that yields a large group-level activation t-statistic can simultaneously fail as an individual-differences biomarker if between-subject variance is small relative to within-subject measurement error—the reliability paradox demonstrated across common task-fMRI paradigms (mean ICC ≈ 0.397; Elliott et al., 2020) and resting-state edges (mean ICC ≈ 0.29; Noble et al., 2019). Choosing between consistency ICC(3,1) and absolute-agreement ICC(2,1) determines whether systematic scanner or longitudinal drift is penalized, while Spearman's attenuation formula shows how reliability ceilings directly cap observable brain-behavior correlations and inflate required sample sizes.

  • Foundational. Shrout PE, Fleiss JL (1979). Intraclass correlations: Uses in assessing rater reliability.. Psychological Bulletin. Cited by 23,390 (OpenAlex); MATCH.

D04 · Plan sample size from effects and precision

Technique: Statistical power, Accuracy in Parameter Estimation (AIPE), Type M/S error design analysis, and group-sequential alpha-spending boundaries

Because reproducible univariate brain-wide association effects are typically small (|r| ≈ 0.06–0.16 even in the top 1%; Marek et al., 2022), traditional neuroimaging studies with n = 20–50 have low power and suffer severe Type M exaggeration (winner's curse) and Type S sign errors whenever selection is conditioned on p < 0.05. Prospective design analysis (Gelman & Carlin, 2014), confidence-interval width planning (AIPE), and pre-specified group-sequential alpha-spending boundaries (Pocock, 1977; O'Brien & Fleming, 1979; Lakens, 2022) allow researchers to size cohorts for precision and conduct interim analyses without inflating Type I error.

D06 · Let the response shape vary: FIR versus a fixed HRF

D08 · Factorial questions and the meaning of an interaction

D09 · From coefficients to a defensible contrast

D10 · Correlated residuals and prewhitening

Technique: fMRI temporal autocorrelation modeling, prewhitening, and generalized least squares (GLS)

First-level fMRI time series exhibit strong positive autocorrelation from slow hemodynamic sluggishness, physiological pulsations, and scanner drift (Purdon & Weisskoff, 1998; Woolrich et al., 2001). Applying ordinary least squares (OLS) without prewhitening underestimates the standard error of slow task designs by several fold, inflating first-level false-positive rates (Eklund et al., 2012; Olszowy et al., 2019). Valid inference requires symmetrical Cholesky prewhitening of both the data y and the design matrix X using an expressive noise covariance model (such as SPM FAST or AFNI 3dREMLfit ARMA(1,1) at short multiband TRs; Corbin et al., 2018; Olszowy et al., 2019).

D11 · People, runs, visits, and hierarchical uncertainty

Technique: Multilevel mixed-effects modeling, repeated-measures hierarchy, and empirical Bayes shrinkage

Neuroimaging datasets are inherently hierarchical, nesting trials within runs, runs within participants, and participants within scanner sites (Beckmann et al., 2003; Woolrich et al., 2004; Chen et al., 2012). Treating repeated runs as independent observations (pseudoreplication; Lazic, 2010) divides variance by N*R instead of N and severely inflates false-positive rates, while scrambling participant IDs in longitudinal contrasts destroys within-subject variance cancellation. Propagating first-level error variances (VARCOPEs) into second-level mixed-effects models (FSL FLAME, AFNI 3dMEMA) and balancing sample size N against scan duration R (Chen et al., 2022; Marek et al., 2022; Kang et al., 2024) are essential for replicable group inference.

D12 · Permutation tests must preserve the experiment

Technique: Nonparametric permutation inference, exchangeability blocks, and cluster/subject bootstrap resampling

When Gaussian random field assumptions fail in neuroimaging, nonparametric permutation tests (in FSL randomise and PALM) provide exact or conservative false-positive control—provided the rearranged configurations are exchangeable under the null hypothesis (Nichols & Holmes, 2002; Winkler et al., 2014). Shuffling rows across repeated runs, twin families, or scanner sites violates exchangeability (requiring multi-level block permutation; Winkler et al., 2015), and omitting the +1 identity count in Monte Carlo permutation p-values yields invalid p = 0 claims (Phipson & Smyth, 2010). Likewise, bootstrap resampling must resample independent participants rather than flat rows and does not make the sample mean robust to extreme outliers.

D13 · Voxel families, clusters, and TFCE are different targets

Technique: Multiple comparisons control: family-wise error rate (FWER), false discovery rate (FDR), random field theory (RFT), and threshold-free cluster enhancement (TFCE)

Whole-brain neuroimaging tests 50,000–200,000 voxels simultaneously, guaranteeing dozens of false positives per map under uncorrected thresholding. Because empirical fMRI spatial autocorrelation has heavier tails than a Gaussian kernel, parametric cluster-extent RFT at lenient thresholds (p < 0.01) inflates FWER up to ~70% (Eklund et al., 2016), making permutation-calibrated max-statistic or TFCE inference essential.

D14 · Region selection and evaluation must be independent

Technique: Circular analysis (double dipping) detection, winner's curse bias, and independent ROI validation

Selecting regions of interest (ROIs) or voxels using the same data—or a statistically dependent contrast—later used to estimate effect sizes or p-values conditions the test statistic on passing a selection threshold, inflating effect estimates via the winner's curse and invalidating inference (Kriegeskorte et al., 2009; Vul et al., 2009). Even within a single GLM run, orthogonal contrast weights are insufficient for independence unless design and noise covariance matrices yield zero estimator covariance.

D15 · Intervals, Bayesian updating, and meaningful effects

Technique: Effect-size estimation, frequentist confidence intervals, conjugate Bayesian updating, and TOST/ROPE equivalence testing

A non-significant p-value (p > 0.05) never proves the absence of an effect; it fails to distinguish a genuinely negligible effect from a noisy, underpowered study (Altman & Bland, 1995; Lakens, 2017). Distinguishing statistical significance from practical importance requires reporting physical-unit effect estimates with confidence or credible intervals (Morey et al., 2016) and testing against a prespecified smallest effect size of interest (SESOI) via Two One-Sided Tests (TOST; Schuirmann, 1987; Lakens, 2018) or Bayesian Region of Practical Equivalence (ROPE; Kruschke, 2018; Linde et al., 2023).

DS09 · Standardization, percent signal change, and reference choices

DS11 · Likelihood: state the noise model before fitting

M05 · PCA reconstruction versus ICA source assumptions

Technique: Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Independent Component Analysis (FastICA) for blind source separation

M06 · Clusters, labels, and stability under changed measurements

M08 · RSA compares relationships, not matching voxel labels

M09 · Searchlight decoding: a center score describes a neighborhood

Spherical and surface-based searchlight mapping (Kriegeskorte et al., 2006; Nilearn, BrainIAK, PyMVPA) is the standard technique for localizing multivariate pattern information across the brain when regions of interest are not fixed a priori. Understanding radius dilation and multi-center false positives prevents researchers from mistaking smoothed sphere footprints for fine-grained cortical boundaries.

M18 · Calibrate probabilities, challenge explanations, preserve the evidence

Technique: Probability calibration (Brier score, ECE, Platt/temperature scaling), permutation feature importance under collinearity, and cryptographic provenance ledgers

Clinical and translational neuroimaging models are frequently judged by rank-based discrimination (ROC-AUC), yet a model with good ROC-AUC can produce severely overconfident risk probabilities that mislead clinical decision thresholds (Van Calster et al., 2019; Guo et al., 2017).
