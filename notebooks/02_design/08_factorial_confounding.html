How to read this scientific diagram & connect it to D08:
In a 2x2 factorial design (Factor A x Factor B), an interaction tests whether the effect of A changes across levels of B: (A2B2 - A1B2) - (A2B1 - A1B1). 2x2 Factorial Interaction Estimand & Coding Invariance

Defines the 2x2 interaction as a symmetric difference-of-differences across the four cell means, invariant to whether the model uses cell-means, dummy, or sum coding.

\[\theta_{A \times B} = \underbrace{(\mu_{11} - \mu_{01})}_{\text{Effect of } A \text{ at } B=1} - \underbrace{(\mu_{10} - \mu_{00})}_{\text{Effect of } A \text{ at } B=0} = \mu_{11} - \mu_{10} - \mu_{01} + \mu_{00} = \mathbf{c}_{\text{int}}^T \boldsymbol{\mu}\]

Why this technique matters

Neuroimaging studies routinely cross cognitive conditions, clinical diagnoses, and scanner sites in factorial designs to test modulation beyond simple cognitive subtraction (Friston et al., 1996). Misinterpreting dummy-coded coefficients as marginal main effects in the presence of an interaction (Schad et al., 2020), pooling unbalanced site-by-diagnosis cells without stratification or harmonization (Fortin et al., 2018), or claiming an interaction because one subgroup has p < 0.05 and another has p > 0.05—an error found in nearly half of surveyed neuroscience papers (Gelman & Stern, 2006; Nieuwenhuis et al., 2011)—leads directly to false scientific conclusions.

Key references · Factorial experimental designs, treatment vs sum/effect contrast coding, multi-site cell-imbalance confounding, and direct interaction testing

  • Foundational — Friston et al. (1996), The Trouble with Cognitive Subtraction, NeuroImage. Critiques the pure insertion assumption of cognitive subtraction and establishes factorial designs and explicit interaction contrasts as the rigorous alternative in neuroimaging.
  • Foundational — Gelman and Stern (2006), The Difference Between “Significant” and “Not Significant” is not Itself Statistically Significant, The American Statistician. Demonstrates mathematically why observing p < 0.05 in one subgroup and p > 0.05 in another does not imply a statistically significant difference or interaction between them.
  • Standard tool — Schad et al. (2020), How to capitalize on a priori contrasts in linear (mixed) models: A tutorial, Journal of Memory and Language. Provides a comprehensive mathematical tutorial on contrast coding matrices, showing why treatment/dummy coding yields simple effects while sum/effect coding recovers true marginal main effects in factorial models.
  • Critical evaluation — Nieuwenhuis et al. (2011), Erroneous analyses of interactions in neuroscience: a problem of significance, Nature Neuroscience. Audits 157 neuroscience papers and shows that nearly half (78/157) committed the difference-in-significance fallacy instead of computing the direct factorial interaction contrast.

1. Scientific & Empirical Motivation: From Published Controversies to Design Mathematics

A factorial experimental design crosses two or more factors—say, Factor \(A \in \{0, 1\}\) (Task Difficulty: Easy vs. Hard) and Factor \(B \in \{0, 1\}\) (Cue Type: Neutral vs. Rewarded, or Scanner Site vs. Clinical Diagnosis)—so that every combination of factor levels \((A, B) \in \{(0,0), (0,1), (1,0), (1,1)\}\) is observed. Crossing factors allows a single study to answer three distinct scientific questions:

  1. Main Effect of Factor \(A\): What is the effect of changing \(A\) from \(0\) to \(1\), averaged across the levels of Factor \(B\)?
  2. Main Effect of Factor \(B\): What is the effect of changing \(B\) from \(0\) to \(1\), averaged across the levels of Factor \(A\)?
  3. Interaction (\(A \times B\)): Does the effect of Factor \(A\) depend on the level of Factor \(B\) (equivalently, does the difference between differences \((\mu_{11} - \mu_{10}) - (\mu_{01} - \mu_{00})\) differ from zero)?

Two major pitfalls in neuroimaging make factorial parameterization and cell balance essential:

  • Coding-Dependent "Main Effects" in Regression Tables: When an interaction column \(A \cdot B\) is included in a regression model, the coefficient \(\beta_A\) on a dummy-coded (\(0/1\)) predictor \(A\) is not the average main effect of \(A\). Instead, \(\beta_A\) represents the simple effect of \(A\) only at the reference level \(B = 0\). Countless neuroimaging and behavioral papers misinterpret a small or non-significant dummy-coded \(\beta_A\) as "no main effect of \(A\)" when an interaction is present.
  • Factorial Confounding & Unbalanced Cells in Multi-Site Studies (PD06, PD07): In Fortin et al. (2018) (PD07) and Kang et al. (2024) (PD06), when biological groups (e.g., Patients vs. Controls, or Young vs. Older participants) are unequally distributed across scanner sites or longitudinal cohorts, unadjusted marginal averages confound biological differences with scanner offsets. Worse, if one cell of a factorial design is completely empty (e.g., Site 2 scans only patients and zero controls), the full factorial model is rank-deficient and the interaction is unidentifiable.

In graduate courses (MIT 9.63 Factorial Design & ANOVA, UC Berkeley Psych 214, and Stanford Psych 204B), we translate seamlessly between cell-mean tables, dummy (\(0/1\)) coding, and centered/effect (\(-0.5/+0.5\)) coding, and reject the widespread fallacy of claiming an interaction merely because one simple effect has \(p < 0.05\) and another has \(p > 0.05\) (Gelman & Stern, 2006; Nieuwenhuis et al., 2011).

2. Mathematical Formulation & Statistical Derivations

2.1 Cell Means, Simple Effects, Marginal Main Effects, and the Interaction Estimand

\[\mu_{00} = 1.0, \qquad \mu_{01} = 3.0, \qquad \mu_{10} = 2.0, \qquad \mu_{11} = 8.0\]

2.2 Dummy (\(0/1\)) Coding vs. Centered (\(-0.5/+0.5\)) Effect Coding

\[\beta_0 = \mu_{00} = 1.0 \quad \text{(Reference cell } A=0, B=0\text{)}\]

2.3 Unbalanced Factorial Cells and Site-by-Group Confounding

What happens in a multi-site clinical neuroimaging study (PD07) when Factor \(A\) is Diagnosis (\(0=\text{Control}, 1=\text{Patient}\)) and Factor \(B\) is Scanner Site (\(0=\text{Site 0}, 1=\text{Site 1}\)), with cell counts \(n_{00}, n_{01}, n_{10}, n_{11}\)? If Site 0 scans \(90\) Controls and \(10\) Patients, while Site 1 scans \(10\) Controls and \(90\) Patients, the naive marginal group difference \(\bar{Y}_{A=1} - \bar{Y}_{A=0}\) weights the cells by their sample proportions rather than \(1/2\), confounding the site offset (\(\bar{\Delta}_B\)) with the diagnosis effect (\(\bar{\Delta}_A\)). By contrast, fitting the full factorial or additive model with cell/site indicators recovers the unconfounded within-site effects—provided every cell has \(n_{ab} > 0\).

Step 1: Core Mechanism — Cell Means, Simple Effects, Marginal Main Effects, and \(0/1\) Dummy Coding

Inspect the four exact condition means \(\mu_{00}=1, \mu_{01}=3, \mu_{10}=2, \mu_{11}=8\) and calculate the simple effects, the equally weighted main effects (\(\bar{\Delta}_A = 3, \bar{\Delta}_B = 4\)), and the difference-of-differences interaction (\(\theta_{AB} = (8-2) - (3-1) = 4\)) before running np.linalg.solve. Notice why \(0/1\) dummy coding reports \(\beta_A = 1.0\) (the simple effect of \(A\) at \(B=0\)) rather than the average main effect \(3.0\).

  1. The fitted cell means are invariant (Xc @ bc == X @ beta).

Step 3: Unbalanced Multi-Site Cohorts & Why "Significant vs. Non-Significant" Is Not an Interaction

We now extend our \(2 \times 2\) design to two critical real-world neuroimaging scenarios:

  1. Unbalanced Cell Counts (PD06, PD07): Suppose \(N = 200\) participants are distributed across Diagnosis \(A \in \{0, 1\}\) and Scanner Site \(B \in \{0, 1\}\) with severe imbalance: \(n_{00} = 80, n_{01} = 20, n_{10} = 20, n_{11} = 80\), and true cell means \(\mu = [1, 3, 2, 8]\). Compare the confounded marginal difference \(\bar{Y}_{A=1} - \bar{Y}_{A=0}\) against the centered factorial OLS estimator.
  2. The Gelman & Stern (2006) Interaction Fallacy: Suppose in Condition \(B=0\) the simple effect of \(A\) is \(\hat{\Delta}_0 = 0.42 \pm 0.20\) (\(t = 2.10, p = 0.038 < 0.05\), "significant"), and in Condition \(B=1\) the simple effect of \(A\) is \(\hat{\Delta}_1 = 0.36 \pm 0.20\) (\(t = 1.80, p = 0.075 > 0.05\), "not significant"). Many papers falsely claim an interaction ("\(A\) activates the ROI in \(B=1\) but not \(B=0\)"), yet the actual interaction test is \(t_{AB} = \frac{0.42 - 0.36}{\sqrt{0.20^2 + 0.20^2}} = 0.212\) (\(p = 0.83\), completely null).

from scipy import stats

# 1. Simulate unbalanced 2x2 cohort (n00=80, n01=20, n10=20, n11=80) with noiseless & noisy outcomes
cell_counts = np.array([80, 20, 20, 80])
A_unbal = np.repeat(A, cell_counts)
B_unbal = np.repeat(B, cell_counts)
y_unbal = np.repeat(y, cell_counts)

naive_marginal_A = y_unbal[A_unbal == 1].mean() - y_unbal[A_unbal == 0].mean()
Xc_unbal = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(y_unbal)), A_unbal - 0.5, B_unbal - 0.5, (A_unbal - 0.5) * (B_unbal - 0.5)])
bc_unbal = np.linalg.lstsq(Xc_unbal, y_unbal, rcond=None)[0]

# In unbalanced data, naive marginal A effect is 6.8 - 1.4 = 5.4 (confounded by B!), while centered factorial recovers 3.0

est_b1, se_b1 = 0.36, 0.20

p_b1 = 2 * stats.t.sf(abs(est_b1 / se_b1), df_sub)

assert p_b0 < 0.05 and p_b1 > 0.05 and p_interaction > 0.80
print(f'Simple effect B=0: p={p_b0:.3f} (<.05) | Simple effect B=1: p={p_b1:.3f} (>.05) | Direct Interaction Test p={p_interaction:.3f} (No interaction!)')

Ask Goose (Qwen) in the spirit of: "Construct a \(2\times 2\) factorial cell table (A in {0,1}, B in {0,1} with cell means [1, 3, 2, 8]) and compare dummy 0/1 coding against centered {-0.5, +0.5} effect coding. Explain why the interaction coefficient \(\beta_{AB} = 4.0\) is identical in both parametrizations while the coefficient on \(A\) changes from \(1.0\) (simple effect at \(B=0\)) to \(3.0\) (average main effect across \(B\)), and demonstrate the Nieuwenhuis et al. (2011) fallacy where \(p_{B=0} = 0.038 < 0.05\) and \(p_{B=1} = 0.075 > 0.05\) despite the true interaction being zero (\(p_{\text{int}} = 0.90\))." Then ask Gemma 4 to audit the physical units, coordinate conventions, and statistical degrees of freedom before you accept the output.

2. What to Look For & How to Trace the Error Back Down

  • Immediate Red Flag: (1) Under 0/1 dummy coding with an interaction term A*B, the researcher reports \(\hat{\beta}_A = 1.0\) as the 'main effect of \(A\)' when the true marginal main effect across \(B \in \{0, 1\}\) is \(3.0\). Or (2) the researcher claims a 'significant group \(\times\) task interaction' because Group 1 had \(p = 0.038\) and Group 2 had \(p = 0.075\), without ever testing \((A_1 - A_0)_{B=1} - (A_1 - A_0)_{B=0}\).
  • Where the Bug Entered: Trace the main-effect discrepancy to partial derivatives of \(E[Y] = \beta_0 + \beta_A A + \beta_B B + \beta_{AB} AB\): when \(B \in \{0, 1\}\), \(\beta_A\) evaluates the effect of \(A\) at \(B = 0\) only; centering \(A_c, B_c \in \{-0.5, +0.5\}\) evaluates \(\beta_{A_c}\) at the midpoint \(\bar{B} = 0\) (or translate via contrast [0, 1, 0, 0.5] @ beta_dummy = 3.0).
  • How to Fix It: Always test interactions directly via \(\mathbf{c}_{\text{int}} = [+1, -1, -1, +1]\).

3. Expected Outcome Range & Why It Must Look That Way

Expected Range: For cell means \((\mu_{00}, \mu_{01}, \mu_{10}, \mu_{11}) = (1, 3, 2, 8)\): dummy 0/1 coding yields \([\beta_0, \beta_A, \beta_B, \beta_{AB}] = [1.0, 1.0, 2.0, 4.0]\), while centered {-0.5, +0.5} coding yields \([3.5, 3.0, 4.0, 4.0]\). In an unbalanced cohort (\(80/20\) vs. \(20/80\)), naive marginal averaging gives a confounded \(A\) difference of \(5.40\), whereas the centered factorial model recovers the exact unweighted main effect \(3.00\). In the Nieuwenhuis demonstration, \(p_{B=0} = 0.038\) and \(p_{B=1} = 0.075\) straddle \(0.05\), yet the direct interaction test gives \(t \approx 0.12, p = 0.905\). Why: The difference between 'significant' (\(p=0.038\)) and 'not significant' (\(p=0.075\)) is not itself statistically significant because \(\text{SE}(\hat{\delta}_1 - \hat{\delta}_0) = \sqrt{\text{SE}_1^2 + \text{SE}_0^2} \approx \sqrt{2}\,\text{SE}\).
