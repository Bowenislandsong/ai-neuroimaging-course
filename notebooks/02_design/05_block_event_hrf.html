Why this technique matters

First-level task-fMRI general linear models in SPM, FSL, AFNI, and Nilearn rely on modeling the sluggish BOLD signal (peaking ~5–6 s post-stimulus) as the linear time-invariant convolution of sub-TR neural event timings with a canonical hemodynamic response function before downsampling to the scanner TR grid. Understanding LTI convolution, low-pass spectral filtering, micro-time slice-timing alignment, and short-ISI vascular nonlinearities prevents catastrophic regressor misalignment (such as unshifted dummy-scan trimming) and explains why block designs maximize detection SNR while rapid event-related designs require careful spacing.

Key references · Neurovascular coupling, linear time-invariant (LTI) hemodynamic convolution, micro-time grid resolution, and frequency-domain design passband analysis

  • Foundational — Boynton et al. (1996), Linear Systems Analysis of Functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging in Human V1, The Journal of Neuroscience. Establishes empirically in human V1 that BOLD fMRI responses behave approximately as a linear time-invariant convolution of neural stimulus timing over multi-second intervals, with sub-additivity at brief durations.
  • Foundational — Logothetis et al. (2001), Neurophysiological investigation of the basis of the fMRI signal, Nature. Demonstrates with simultaneous intracortical physiology and fMRI that the BOLD impulse response reflects local field potentials (synaptic input/integrative processing) more closely than multi-unit spiking. Course library: PB01.
  • Standard tool — Glover (1999), Deconvolution of Impulse Response in Event-Related BOLD fMRI1, NeuroImage. Characterizes the double-gamma hemodynamic impulse response and shows that linear deconvolution holds when stimuli are separated by >=4 s but breaks down under rapid overlap.
  • Review — Polimeni and Lewis (2021), Imaging faster neural dynamics with fast fMRI: A need for updated models of the hemodynamic response, Progress in Neurobiology. Reviews fast-fMRI vascular physics and demonstrates why standard space- and time-invariant canonical HRF assumptions require revision at sub-second timescales.

Full entries, verification status and citation counts: course bibliography.

1. Scientific & Empirical Motivation: From Published Controversies to Design Mathematics

A BIDS *_events.tsv file records experimental stimuli and participant actions (onset and duration in seconds relative to the first retained scanner trigger), whereas an fMRI time series records T2*-weighted Blood-Oxygenation-Level-Dependent (BOLD) contrast sampled once every repetition time (\(\text{TR}\), typically \(0.8\text{–}2.0\text{ s}\)). Bridging the gap between a discrete psychological event log and a sampled hemodynamic time series requires an explicit forward biophysical model.

Three foundational papers ground this forward model and its limits:

  • Logothetis et al. (2001) (PB01) combined simultaneous intracortical microelectrode recordings with fMRI in primate visual cortex, demonstrating that the BOLD signal primarily reflects perisynaptic local field potentials (LFPs—dendritic input and integrative processing) rather than output spiking alone, and acts as a sluggish low-pass temporal filter delayed by \(4\text{–}6\text{ s}\) and dispersed over \(15\text{–}25\text{ s}\). Furthermore, neurovascular coupling exhibits sub-additive nonlinearity (vascular refractory and neural adaptation effects) when brief stimuli are separated by less than \(2\text{–}4\text{ s}\).
  • Detection Power vs. Individual-Difference Reliability: While block designs substantially increase within-run detection power compared to rapid event-related designs (Friston et al., 1999; Liu et al., 2001), Elliott et al. (2020) (PD03) found in their meta-analysis that task design (block vs event-related), task length, and retest interval did not significantly moderate test-retest reliability—showing that high group-level detection SNR alone does not guarantee reliable individual differences.
  • Timescale Filtering Across Modalities: Electrophysiological and MEG hidden Markov model studies resolve transient network states on sub-second timescales (\(100\text{--}300\text{ ms}\)), whereas in resting-state fMRI (Vidaurre et al., 2017, PM08) the sluggish hemodynamic response (\(\sim 5\text{--}6\text{ s}\) peak) and TR sampling restrict observable dynamics to slower metastate transitions.

In graduate fMRI courses (Stanford Psych 204B, Dartmouth DartBrains, Oxford FSL FEAT, and UCL SPM), we formalize the Linear Time-Invariant (LTI) Hemodynamic Forward Model, trace how sustained blocks and brief impulses are transformed in both the time and frequency domains, and enforce strict temporal alignment between micro-time event grids, discarded dummy volumes, slice-timing reference offsets, and TR-sampled design matrices.

2. Mathematical Formulation & Statistical Derivations

2.1 Continuous and Discrete Linear Time-Invariant (LTI) Convolution

Let \(s(t) \ge 0\) denote the neural stimulus input function in continuous time \(t \in [0, T]\) (expressed in seconds from the start of the retained scan), and let \(h(\tau)\) denote the Hemodynamic Response Function (HRF)—the theoretical BOLD response to an instantaneous unit impulse \(\delta(t)\) at \(t = 0\). Under the LTI assumptions of:

  1. Scaling (Homogeneity): Input \(\alpha s(t)\) produces response \(\alpha x(t)\),
  2. Time Invariance: Input \(s(t - t_0)\) produces response \(x(t - t_0)\),
  3. Superposition (Additivity): Input \(s_1(t) + s_2(t)\) produces response \(x_1(t) + x_2(t)\),

the predicted continuous BOLD signal \(x(t)\) is given by the causal convolution integral:

\[x(t) = (s * h)(t) = \int_0^t s(\tau) \, h(t - \tau) \, d\tau = \int_0^\infty s(t - \tau) \, h(\tau) \, d\tau\]

On a discrete micro-time grid \(t_m = m \Delta t\) (\(m = 0, 1, \dots, M-1\), where \(\Delta t = 1\text{ s}\) in our introductory grid or \(\Delta t = \text{TR}/16\) in software oversampling), discrete causal convolution truncates to the first \(M\) time points (np.convolve(s, h)[:M]):

\[x[m] = \sum_{k=0}^{L_h - 1} s[m - k] \, h[k], \qquad s[m - k] = 0 \text{ for } m < k\]

2.2 Double-Gamma Canonical HRF Parameterization

Following Boynton et al. (1996), Friston et al. (1998), and Glover (1999), the canonical HRF \(h(t)\) is modeled as the difference of two Gamma probability density functions—a positive hemodynamic peak (\(a_1 = 6\), peaking around \(5\text{ s}\)) minus a smaller, slower post-stimulus undershoot (\(a_2 = 16\), peaking around \(15\text{ s}\), weighted by \(1/c = 1/6\)):

\[h_{\text{raw}}(t) = f_{\Gamma}(t; a_1, 1) - \frac{1}{c} f_{\Gamma}(t; a_2, 1) = \frac{t^{a_1 - 1} e^{-t}}{\Gamma(a_1)} - \frac{1}{6} \frac{t^{a_2 - 1} e^{-t}}{\Gamma(a_2)}, \qquad t \ge 0\]

In our teaching implementation on a \(1\text{ s}\) grid (hrf_t = np.arange(32)), we normalize \(h(t) = h_{\text{raw}}(t) / \max_\tau h_{\text{raw}}(\tau)\) so that a single \(1\text{ s}\) unit impulse produces a peak height of \(1.0\) at lag \(\tau_{\text{peak}} = 5\text{ s}\).

2.3 Sustained Blocks vs. Brief Impulses and Frequency-Domain Filtering

Why does a \(20\text{ s}\) sustained block (blocks[10:30] = 1) reach a plateau amplitude \(\approx 5.433\times\) higher than a single \(1\text{ s}\) impulse (events[10] = 1)? For a sustained boxcar of duration \(D\), the convolved response at time \(t\) after onset is the cumulative sum (step response) of the HRF:

\[x_{\text{block}}(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{\min(t, D-1)} h[k] \xrightarrow{D \gg \tau_{\text{peak}}} \sum_{k=0}^{L_h - 1} h[k] \approx 3.32\]

Because \(|\mathcal{F}\{h\}(\omega)|\) is a band-pass / low-pass filter peaking near \(f \approx 0.03\text{–}0.04\text{ Hz}\) and attenuating frequencies above \(0.15\text{ Hz}\), alternating \(20\text{ s}\) blocks (\(f_0 \approx 1/60\text{–}1/40\text{ Hz}\)) place almost all their spectral power directly inside the HRF passband, maximizing detection power.

2.4 Scanner TR Downsampling, Micro-Time Oversampling, and Dummy-Scan Alignment

An fMRI scanner acquires one volume every \(\text{TR}\) seconds (here \(\text{TR} = 2\text{ s}\), yielding \(N_{\text{scans}} = 160 / 2 = 80\) volumes). Crucially:

  1. Convolve First, Sample Second: Convolution must be computed on the fine temporal grid \(t \in [0, T)\) before slicing at scan acquisition times \(t_n = n \cdot \text{TR}\) (pred_event[::tr]). Downsampling the stimulus train before convolution aliases or completely drops events that do not land on multiples of \(\text{TR}\).
  2. Discarded Dummy Scans Shift the Temporal Origin: If the first \(K_{\text{dummy}}\) volumes (\(T_{\text{discard}} = K_{\text{dummy}} \cdot \text{TR}\) seconds, e.g., \(4\text{ s}\) for \(2\) dummy scans) are discarded for T1 longitudinal magnetization equilibrium, every event onset \(t_{\text{onset}}\) logged relative to the very first scanner trigger must be shifted by:

\[t_{\text{onset}}^{\text{retained}} = t_{\text{onset}}^{\text{raw}} - K_{\text{dummy}} \cdot \text{TR}\]

Merely slicing pred_event[2:] without verifying how onsets were referenced—or relabeling scan indices without shifting the underlying time axis—misaligns the predicted BOLD peak against every acquired fMRI volume.

Step 1: Core Mechanism — Double-Gamma HRF, Impulse vs. Block Convolution, and LTI Superposition

Predict the peak lag of the double-gamma HRF (stats.gamma.pdf(hrf_t, 6) - stats.gamma.pdf(hrf_t, 16) / 6) and compare the convolved response to isolated impulses ([10, 70, 110]), closely spaced overlapping impulses ([30, 32]), and sustained \(20\text{ s}\) blocks ([10:30] and [70:90]). Verify causality (pred_event[:10] == 0) and exact LTI superposition (convolve(events + blocks, h) == pred_event + pred_block).

Python · Executable Cell 1
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy import stats

# 1. Construct 1-second internal time grid (160 s) and peak-normalized double-gamma HRF (32 s support)
seconds = np.arange(160)
hrf_t = np.arange(32)
h = stats.gamma.pdf(hrf_t, 6) - stats.gamma.pdf(hrf_t, 16) / 6
h /= h.max()

# 2. Define neural stimulus trains: brief events (including rapid pair at 30s, 32s) vs 20s sustained blocks
events = np.zeros(160)
events[[10, 30, 32, 70, 110]] = 1
blocks = np.zeros(160)
blocks[10:30] = 1
blocks[70:90] = 1

# 3. Causal LTI convolution truncated to run duration (160 seconds)
pred_event = np.convolve(events, h)[:160]
pred_block = np.convolve(blocks, h)[:160]

assert h.argmax() == 5
assert np.allclose(pred_event[:10], 0)
assert np.allclose(np.convolve(events + blocks, h)[:160], pred_event + pred_block)
print('Teaching HRF peak lag:', h.argmax(), 'seconds')
print(f'Single event peak (at t=15s): {pred_event[15]:.3f} | Overlapping pair peak (at t=36s): {pred_event[36]:.3f} | 20s Block plateau peak: {pred_block.max():.3f}')
Teaching HRF peak lag: 5 seconds
Single event peak (at t=15s): 1.000 | Overlapping pair peak (at t=36s): 1.799 | 20s Block plateau peak: 5.433

Step 2: Scanner TR Sampling and Dummy-Volume Origin Alignment

An fMRI GLM design matrix has one row per retained scanner volume (\(N_{\text{scans}} = 80\) at \(\text{TR} = 2\text{ s}\)). Below we sample the continuous \(1\text{ s}\) prediction every \(\text{TR} = 2\text{ s}\) (pred_event[::tr]) and demonstrate how discarding \(2\) initial dummy scans (\(4\text{ s}\)) requires shifting every event onset backward by \(4\text{ s}\) (events[4:]) before convolution and TR slicing. We also audit the deliberate mistake of shifting only the plot axis or array length without shifting the event timing.

# 1. Sample convolved predictions at scanner repetition time TR = 2 seconds (80 volumes)
tr = 2
scan_times = seconds[::tr]

sampled_block = pred_block[::tr]

Step 3: Sub-Second Micro-Time Oversampling & Frequency-Domain Passband Analysis

Real fMRI experiments often present stimuli at jittered sub-second onsets (e.g., \(t = 10.35\text{ s}, 24.75\text{ s}\)) and acquire slices across the volume over \([0, \text{TR})\). Standard fMRI packages (SPM, Nilearn, FSL) construct a micro-time grid with step \(\Delta t = \text{TR} / 16 = 0.125\text{ s}\), convolve on that fine grid, and sample at a specific slice-time reference (slice_time_ref \(\in [0, 1]\)). Below we compare micro-time oversampling against naive TR-binning, and compute the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) power spectra \(|\mathcal{F}\{s\}(\omega)|\) and \(|\mathcal{F}\{h\}(\omega)|\) to show why block designs pass through the HRF filter with much higher power than rapid events.

Python · Executable Cell 3
# 1. Sub-second micro-time oversampling (dt = 0.125 s, 16 bins per TR=2s) vs naive TR rounding
oversample = 16
dt = tr / oversample
t_micro = np.arange(0, 160, dt)
h_micro = stats.gamma.pdf(t_micro[:int(32 / dt)], 6) - stats.gamma.pdf(t_micro[:int(32 / dt)], 16) / 6
h_micro = h_micro / h_micro.max()  # Peak-normalized to 1.0 to match Step 1 impulse scale

subsec_onsets = np.array([10.35, 30.60, 32.85, 70.25, 110.75])
s_micro = np.zeros_like(t_micro)
s_micro[np.round(subsec_onsets / dt).astype(int)] = 1.0
conv_micro = np.convolve(s_micro, h_micro)[:len(t_micro)]

# Slice-timing reference: sample at beginning of TR (ref=0.0) vs middle of TR (ref=0.5 -> +1.0s)
pred_ref_start = conv_micro[0::oversample]
pred_ref_mid = conv_micro[(oversample // 2)::oversample]
rms_slice_shift = np.sqrt(np.mean((pred_ref_mid - pred_ref_start) ** 2))
assert pred_ref_start.shape == (80,) and rms_slice_shift > 0.01

# 2. Frequency-domain analysis: FFT amplitude spectra of HRF, Block train, and Event train
freqs = np.fft.rfftfreq(len(seconds), d=1.0)
fft_hrf = np.abs(np.fft.rfft(h, n=len(seconds)))
fft_hrf /= fft_hrf.max()
fft_block_in = np.abs(np.fft.rfft(blocks - blocks.mean()))
fft_event_in = np.abs(np.fft.rfft(events - events.mean()))

# Fraction of stimulus spectral energy falling inside the HRF low-pass passband (f <= 0.06 Hz)
passband = freqs <= 0.06
block_pass_frac = np.sum(fft_block_in[passband] ** 2) / np.sum(fft_block_in ** 2)
event_pass_frac = np.sum(fft_event_in[passband] ** 2) / np.sum(fft_event_in ** 2)
assert block_pass_frac > 2.0 * event_pass_frac
print(f'Spectral energy in HRF passband (<=0.06 Hz) -> Block design: {100*block_pass_frac:.1f}% vs. Event design: {100*event_pass_frac:.1f}%')
Spectral energy in HRF passband (<=0.06 Hz) -> Block design: 87.3% vs. Event design: 14.5%

2. What to Look For & How to Trace the Error Back Down

  • Immediate Red Flag: Correlation between the predicted regressor and actual BOLD response drops from \(1.000\) to \(\approx 0.35\), OR all event durations (\(0.5\text{ s}\) vs. \(4.0\text{ s}\)) produce identical regressor amplitudes because the script ignored the BIDS duration column and treated every event as a unit impulse.
  • Where the Bug Entered: Trace the \(r = 0.35\) drop to a \(4\text{ s}\) onset clock mismatch (such as failing to subtract discarded dummy-scan time \(N_{\text{dummy}} \times \text{TR}\) or omitting HRF convolution entirely).
  • How to Fix It: Always build the neural indicator on a fine sub-second grid (\(\Delta t \approx 0.1\text{ s}\)) incorporating both onset and duration, convolve with \(h(t)\), and then sample at the exact slice/volume timestamps.

3. Expected Outcome Range & Why It Must Look That Way

Expected Range: With unit impulse peak normalized to \(1.00\), two impulses \(2\text{ s}\) apart linearly sum to peak \(\approx 1.80\), and a sustained \(20\text{ s}\) block saturates to a plateau of \(\approx 5.43\). A \(4\text{ s}\) timing offset drops regressor correlation from \(1.000\) to \(\approx 0.35\). In frequency space, an alternating block design concentrates \(\approx 85\text{–}90\%\) of its spectral energy inside the HRF passband (\(\le 0.06\text{ Hz}\)), whereas a rapid event schedule places only \(\approx 12\text{–}18\%\) in that passband. Why: The hemodynamic response \(h(t)\) acts as a sluggish linear time-invariant (LTI) low-pass filter (bandwidth \(\lesssim 0.06\text{ Hz}\)), integrating sustained low-frequency blocks to \(\int h(\tau)\,d\tau\) while attenuating rapid high-frequency transitions.
