Why this technique matters

Every neuroimaging analysis—from multi-team task-fMRI pipelines (NARPS) to biobank brain-wide association studies (UK Biobank, ABCD)—implicitly targets a specific causal or descriptive estimand before any regression or machine-learning estimator is chosen. Without explicitly specifying the unit-level potential outcomes, target population, and confounder set on a causal DAG, researchers conflate analytical flexibility that changes the scientific question (e.g., conditioning on colliders or altering parametric modulators) with mere estimator variance, and risk severe bias when covariate overlap (positivity) fails across sites or clinical groups.

Key references · Causal estimands, potential outcomes, DAG identification, and inverse-probability / standardization adjustment

  • Foundational — Rubin (1974), Estimating causal effects of treatments in randomized and nonrandomized studies., Journal of Educational Psychology. Formalizes causal effects as comparisons of potential outcomes Y(1) and Y(0) on a common set of units, establishing why observational comparisons require explicit assignment-mechanism assumptions.
  • Foundational — Rosenbaum and Rubin (1983), The central role of the propensity score in observational studies for causal effects, Biometrika. Introduces the propensity score e(X)=P(A=1|X) and proves that conditioning or weighting on e(X) suffices to remove confounding from observed covariates X under strong ignorability and positivity.
  • Critical evaluation — Lundberg et al. (2021), What Is Your Estimand? Defining the Target Quantity Connects Statistical Evidence to Theory, American Sociological Review. Shows why researchers must state the unit-specific quantity, target population, and counterfactual comparison before choosing an estimator or regression specification.
  • Review — Hernán and Robins (2006), Estimating causal effects from epidemiological data, Journal of Epidemiology and Community Health. Clearly links potential-outcome estimands, exchangeability, positivity, and consistency to inverse-probability weighting and standardization in observational studies.

1. Scientific & Empirical Motivation: From Published Controversies to Design Mathematics

An estimand is the precise mathematical quantity a scientific investigation intends to learn before a single line of regression code is executed. It specifies four non-negotiable components:

  1. Target Population (\(\mathcal{P}\)): Whose brains and behavior are being summarized (e.g., community-dwelling adults aged 18–35 vs. a clinically enriched hospital convenience cohort)?
  2. Outcome Variable (\(Y\)): What physical or physiological signal is measured, in what units, and over what spatial and temporal support (e.g., percent BOLD signal change in a prespecified anatomical parcel during task condition \(A\) vs. \(B\))?
  3. Intervention or Comparison (\(A \in \{0, 1\}\)): What well-defined manipulation or exposure is contrasted, and what is the unit of assignment (individual participant, scanning session, or trial)?
  4. Population Summary / Averaging Rule: How individual-level contrasts are aggregated across the target population (e.g., an equally weighted expectation \(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}}[Y(1) - Y(0)]\) vs. a stratum-weighted or precision-weighted average).

Why must neuroimaging researchers formalize estimands before calling np.linalg.lstsq or fitting a general linear model (GLM)? In Botvinik-Nezer et al. (2020) (PD01), the Neuroimaging Analysis Replication and Prediction Study (NARPS) distributed the exact same fMRI dataset (\(N = 108\) participants performing a mixed-gamble decision task) to 70 independent analysis teams. Across nine binary hypotheses, teams reached strikingly different conclusions—not merely because they chose different software packages or spatial smoothing kernels (estimator differences), but because several pipeline choices implicitly altered the estimand itself (such as how behavioral risk/loss sensitivity was parameterized, which trials or participants were excluded, and how first-level contrasts were defined). Similarly, in Kang et al. (2024) (PD06), enriching a Brain-Wide Association Study (BWAS) sample for extreme phenotypes changes the variance of the covariate distribution and therefore changes standardized correlation estimands unless population transport weights are explicitly applied.

In graduate neuroimaging curricula—such as MIT 9.63 (Laboratory in Visual Cognition), Stanford Psych 204B (Poldrack), and Hernán & Robins' Causal Inference: What If—we separate three distinct layers of scientific inquiry:

  • Causal Estimand Layer: Defined on potential outcomes \((Y_i(0), Y_i(1))\) in a target population \(\mathcal{P}\).
  • Identification Layer: The mathematical assumptions (Consistency, Conditional Exchangeability / Ignorability, and Positivity) that link unobserved counterfactuals to the distribution of observed data \((X_i, A_i, Y_i)\).
  • Estimation Layer: The finite-sample algorithm (ordinary least squares, inverse probability weighting, standardization/g-formula) applied to an observed sample of size \(n\).

Asking an AI coding assistant to "test whether group A differs from group B, controlling for site" conflates all three layers. If treatment or group assignment is deterministically confounded with baseline stratum or scanner site (a positivity violation), no regression adjustment can separate the biological effect from the stratum offset, even when linear algebra libraries return finite least-squares numbers without raising an exception.

2. Mathematical Formulation & Statistical Derivations

2.1 Rubin Causal Model and Potential Outcomes

Let \(i \in \{1, \dots, N\}\) index independent participants drawn from a population \(\mathcal{P}\). For a binary intervention \(A_i \in \{0, 1\}\), each participant possesses a pair of potential outcomes:

  • \(Y_i(0) \in \mathbb{R}\): the neuroimaging response that would be observed if participant \(i\) were assigned to control (\(A_i = 0\)).
  • \(Y_i(1) \in \mathbb{R}\): the neuroimaging response that would be observed if participant \(i\) were assigned to active intervention (\(A_i = 1\)).

The individual causal effect for person \(i\) is \(\tau_i = Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)\). By the Fundamental Problem of Causal Inference, at any given measurement occasion we observe only the realized outcome under the actually assigned condition. Under the Consistency and No-Interference (SUTVA) assumptions, the observed outcome \(Y_i\) is connected to the potential outcomes via the switching equation:

\[Y_i = A_i Y_i(1) + (1 - A_i) Y_i(0)\]

2.2 Average Treatment Effect (ATE) and Stratum Heterogeneity

Suppose each participant belongs to a baseline covariate stratum \(X_i \in \{-1, +1\}\) (for example, low vs. high baseline vascular reactivity, or Scanner Site 1 vs. Scanner Site 2). In our synthetic data-generating process (DGP) of \(N = 200\) individuals (100 with \(X_i = -1\) and 100 with \(X_i = +1\)), the potential outcomes follow:

\[Y_i(0) = 3 X_i, \qquad \tau(X_i) = 0.5 + 0.2 X_i, \qquad Y_i(1) = Y_i(0) + \tau(X_i) = 0.5 + 3.2 X_i\]

Notice that the causal effect is heterogeneous across strata (effect modification):

  • In the low-baseline stratum (\(X_i = -1\)): \(Y_i(0) = -3.0\), \(\tau(-1) = 0.5 + 0.2(-1) = 0.3\), and \(Y_i(1) = -2.7\).
  • In the high-baseline stratum (\(X_i = +1\)): \(Y_i(0) = +3.0\), \(\tau(+1) = 0.5 + 0.2(+1) = 0.7\), and \(Y_i(1) = +3.7\).

The Population Average Treatment Effect (ATE) over a target population \(\mathcal{P}\) with stratum probabilities \(w(-1) = \mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{P}}(X = -1)\) and \(w(+1) = \mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{P}}(X = +1)\) is:

\[\theta_{\text{ATE}}(\mathcal{P}) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}}[Y(1) - Y(0)] = \sum_{x \in \{-1, +1\}} w(x) \, \mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid X = x]\]

When both strata are equally represented (\(w(-1) = w(+1) = 0.5\)), \(\theta_{\text{ATE}} = 0.5(0.3) + 0.5(0.7) = 0.50\text{ outcome units}\).

2.3 Selection Bias Decomposition and Positivity Failure

Why does the naive observed-group difference \(\hat{\Delta}_{\text{obs}} = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid A = 1] - \mathbb{E}[Y \mid A = 0]\) fail under observational confounding? Adding and subtracting the counterfactual term \(\mathbb{E}[Y(0) \mid A = 1]\) yields the exact decomposition:

\[\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[Y \mid A = 1] - \mathbb{E}[Y \mid A = 0]}_{\text{Observed Association } \Delta_{\text{obs}}} = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid A = 1]}_{\text{Average Treatment Effect on the Treated (ATT)}} + \underbrace{\big(\mathbb{E}[Y(0) \mid A = 1] - \mathbb{E}[Y(0) \mid A = 0]\big)}_{\text{Baseline Confounding / Selection Bias}}\]

In our observational allocation (\(A_i^{\text{obs}} = \mathbb{I}(X_i > 0)\)), only high-baseline participants receive \(A = 1\) and only low-baseline participants receive \(A = 0\). Consequently:

  • \(\text{ATT} = \mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid X = +1] = 0.7\).
  • \(\text{Baseline Selection Bias} = \mathbb{E}[Y(0) \mid X = +1] - \mathbb{E}[Y(0) \mid X = -1] = 3.0 - (-3.0) = 6.0\).
  • \(\Delta_{\text{obs}} = 0.7 + 6.0 = 6.7\text{ units}\)—a 13.4-fold distortion of the true ATE (\(0.5\)).

Can we adjust for \(X\) using standardization (the g-formula) or Inverse Probability of Treatment Weighting (IPTW)? Both require two identification conditions:

  1. Conditional Exchangeability (Ignorability): \((Y(0), Y(1)) \perp\!\!\!\perp A \mid X\).
  2. Positivity (Overlap): For every stratum \(x\) with \(\mathbb{P}(X = x) > 0\), the propensity score \(e(x) = \mathbb{P}(A = 1 \mid X = x)\) must satisfy:

\[0 < e(x) < 1\]

Under the observational allocation \(A_i^{\text{obs}} = \mathbb{I}(X_i > 0)\), we have \(e(-1) = 0\) and \(e(+1) = 1\), violating positivity everywhere. In linear algebra terms, the design matrix \(D = [\mathbf{1}_N, A^{\text{obs}}, X] \in \mathbb{R}^{200 \times 3}\) satisfies \(X = 2 A^{\text{obs}} - \mathbf{1}_N\), so \(\text{rank}(D) = 2 < 3\). The normal equations \((D^\top D)\beta = D^\top Y\) are singular; pseudoinverse solvers (np.linalg.lstsq) return the minimum-norm vector in an infinite affine subspace of solutions, which does not identify the causal effect.

2.4 Target-Population Transport and Propensity Weighting Under Overlap

When positivity holds (\(0 < e(X_i) < 1\)) and conditional exchangeability holds, the Horvitz-Thompson Inverse Probability of Treatment Weighting (IPTW) estimator consistently recovers the ATE:

\[\hat{\theta}_{\text{IPTW}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \left( \frac{A_i Y_i}{e(X_i)} - \frac{(1 - A_i) Y_i}{1 - e(X_i)} \right)\]

Step 1: Core Mechanism — Potential Outcomes, Confounded Assignment, and Exact Balance

Before running the code below, predict both the true population Average Treatment Effect (\(\theta_{\text{ATE}}\)) and the naive observed-group difference (\(\hat{\Delta}_{\text{obs}}\)) under the deterministic observational assignment \(A_i = \mathbb{I}(X_i > 0)\). Notice how the switching equation \(Y_i = A_i Y_i(1) + (1 - A_i) Y_i(0)\) conceals half of the potential outcome table in any real empirical dataset, and why exact within-stratum balance (balanced = np.tile([0, 1], 100)) eliminates baseline confounding (\(3.0 - (-3.0) = 6.0\)) to recover \(\theta_{\text{ATE}} = 0.50\).

y1 = y0 + effect

# 2. Compare deterministic confounded allocation vs. exact within-stratum balanced allocation
observational = (x > 0).astype(int)
balanced = np.tile([0, 1], 100)

    y = np.where(assignment == 1, y1, y0)
    return y[assignment == 1].mean() - y[assignment == 0].mean()

Step 2: Target-Population Transport and Rank-Deficiency Audit

Now hold the individual potential outcomes \((Y_i(0), Y_i(1))\) fixed, and change the target population \(\mathcal{P}^*\) so that 80% of the target population belongs to the high-baseline stratum (\(X = +1\)) and 20% belongs to the low-baseline stratum (\(X = -1\)). Because treatment effects are heterogeneous (\(\tau(-1)=0.3\) vs. \(\tau(+1)=0.7\)), changing the population weights changes the true causal estimand from \(0.50\) to \(0.62\). Step 3: Propensity Score Overlap Sweep — When Does Statistical Adjustment Succeed?

Between the two extremes of deterministic confounding (\(e(+1) = 1.0, e(-1) = 0.0\)) and perfect 50/50 balance (\(e(+1) = 0.5, e(-1) = 0.5\)), real neuroimaging cohorts often exhibit partial confounding (e.g., 85% of high-stratum participants and 15% of low-stratum participants receive \(A=1\)). As long as positivity holds (\(0 < e(x) < 1\)), both within-stratum standardization (the g-formula) and Inverse Probability of Treatment Weighting (IPTW) are mathematically unbiased, whereas the unadjusted group contrast suffers severe confounding bias. However, as \(e(+1) \to 1\), the inverse weights \(1/(1 - e(x))\) explode, inflating finite-sample estimation variance.

Python · Executable Cell 3
# Parametric sweep across propensity score imbalance p_high = P(A=1 | X=+1), with P(A=1 | X=-1) = 1 - p_high
rng = np.random.default_rng(201)
p_grid = np.array([0.50, 0.65, 0.80, 0.90, 0.96])
n_mc = 400
naive_means, iptw_means, iptw_sds, gform_sds = [], [], [], []

for p_hi in p_grid:
    p_x = np.where(x > 0, p_hi, 1.0 - p_hi)
    mc_naive, mc_iptw, mc_gform = [], [], []
    for _ in range(n_mc):
        # Ensure at least one treated and untreated unit per stratum for finite-sample g-formula
        a_sim = rng.binomial(1, p_x)
        y_sim = np.where(a_sim == 1, y1, y0) + rng.normal(0, 0.5, size=len(x))
        mc_naive.append(y_sim[a_sim == 1].mean() - y_sim[a_sim == 0].mean())
        # Horvitz-Thompson IPTW estimator using true propensity scores
        iptw_est = np.mean(a_sim * y_sim / p_x - (1 - a_sim) * y_sim / (1 - p_x))
        mc_iptw.append(iptw_est)
        # Standardization / OLS with stratum indicator on full-rank design
        X_mat = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(x)), a_sim, x])
        b_ols = np.linalg.lstsq(X_mat, y_sim, rcond=None)[0]
        mc_gform.append(b_ols[1])
    naive_means.append(np.mean(mc_naive))
    iptw_means.append(np.mean(mc_iptw))
    iptw_sds.append(np.std(mc_iptw, ddof=1))
    gform_sds.append(np.std(mc_gform, ddof=1))

sweep_df = pd.DataFrame({
    'P(A=1|X=+1)': p_grid,
    'Naive_Mean': np.round(naive_means, 3),
    'IPTW_Mean': np.round(iptw_means, 3),
    'IPTW_SD': np.round(iptw_sds, 3),
    'Adjusted_OLS_SD': np.round(gform_sds, 3),
})
assert np.all(np.abs(np.array(iptw_means) - 0.5) < 0.08)
assert iptw_sds[-1] > 2.0 * iptw_sds[0] and gform_sds[-1] > 2.0 * gform_sds[0]
print(sweep_df.to_string(index=False))

2. What to Look For & How to Trace the Error Back Down

  • Immediate Red Flag: The AI reports the naive observational difference (6.70) as the treatment effect when the true causal effect is 0.50, OR runs np.linalg.lstsq on a design where all patients were scanned at Site 1 and all controls at Site 2 (\(A \equiv X\)) and interprets the resulting split coefficient (2.25 instead of 0.50) as 'adjusted for site'.
  • Where the Bug Entered: Always check the covariate-by-group contingency table (pd.crosstab(X, A)) and np.linalg.matrix_rank(D) before interpreting regression weights.
  • How to Fix It: When \(P(A=1 \mid X) \in \{0, 1\}\) (positivity violation), the columns for Group and Site are collinear (rank(D) < p); minimum-norm lstsq arbitrarily splits the sum \((\beta_{\text{group}} + \beta_{\text{site}})\) across both columns without separating them.

3. Expected Outcome Range & Why It Must Look That Way

Expected Range: True population ATE is \(0.50\) (\(0.30\) in \(X=0\), \(0.70\) in \(X=1\)); naive confounded difference is \(6.70\) (\(= 0.70\text{ ATT} + 6.00\text{ baseline bias}\)). Across moderate confounding regimes (\(P(A=1\mid X=+1) \in [0.50, 0.80]\) where positivity holds), naive difference drifts from \(0.54\) to \(4.22\), whereas both IPTW and stratum-adjusted OLS stay unbiased in \([0.47, 0.55]\) (with OLS having \(\sim 6\times\) smaller SD than IPTW: \(\approx 0.08\) vs. \(0.46\)). When positivity fails (\(A \equiv X\)), lstsq silently outputs \(\hat{\beta}_A \approx 2.25\) instead of \(0.50\). Why: Adjustment requires positive overlap \(0 < P(A=1 \mid X=x) < 1\) in every confounder stratum so both potential outcomes are observed within each stratum.
