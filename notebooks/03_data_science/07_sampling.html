Sampling Distributions, Temporal Autocorrelation & Effective Sample Size (N_eff)

How to read this scientific diagram & connect it to DS07:
The standard error of a mean shrinks as sigma / sqrt(N) only when observations are independent. In fMRI time series with positive autocorrelation rho_1, adjacent TRs carry redundant information, shrinking the effective sample size N_eff and widening the true sampling distribution.

Governing Mathematical Formulation & Term-by-Term Logic

Autocorrelation-Adjusted Effective Sample Size & Standard Error

Reduces the nominal number of time points T by the integrated autocorrelation time to reflect the true number of independent degrees of freedom.

\[T_{\text{eff}} = \frac{T}{1 + 2\sum_{k=1}^{T-1}\left(1 - \frac{k}{T}\right)\rho_k} \xrightarrow{\text{AR(1)}} T \cdot \frac{1 - \rho_1}{1 + \rho_1}, \qquad \text{SE}(\bar{y}) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{T_{\text{eff}}}}\]
Logical Term Role in Equation What This Part Is Doing (Mechanism & Units) What Breaks If Omitted or Mis-Specified (Failure Mode)
\(\rho_k = \operatorname{Corr}(y_t, y_{t-k})\)Lag-k Temporal AutocorrelationCorrelation between fMRI samples separated by k TRs; positive due to the sluggish ~6 s hemodynamic response.Treating 1,000 multiband TRs (TR=0.5 s) as 1,000 independent draws when computing correlation p-values.
\(\frac{1 - \rho_1}{1 + \rho_1}\)AR(1) Information Reduction FactorAt lag-1 autocorrelation rho_1 = 0.6, each TR provides only (0.4/1.6) = 25% of an independent observation!Using unadjusted sqrt(T - 2) in Fisher-z standard errors 1/sqrt(T - 3), wildly inflating connectivity significance.

Why this technique matters

Brain-wide association studies (BWAS) typically target small true correlations (|r| ~ 0.05-0.15) using multi-site or family-clustered cohorts (ABCD, HCP, UK Biobank). At small sample sizes (N = 25), significance filtering guarantees at least a 3.96x magnitude exaggeration (Type M error / winner's curse) and ~10% sign flips (Type S error), while ignoring within-participant or twin intraclass correlation (ICC) inflates naive false-positive rates from 5% tonearly 29%.

Key references · Sampling variability, Kish's clustered design effect (DEFF) and effective sample size, and Type M / Type S errors

  • Foundational — Gelman and Carlin (2014), Beyond Power Calculations, Perspectives on Psychological Science. Formalizes Type M (exaggeration ratio / winner's curse) and Type S (sign) errors when filtering noisy, underpowered estimates by statistical significance thresholds.
  • Standard tool — Winkler et al. (2015), Multi-level block permutation, NeuroImage. Introduces multi-level block exchangeability for clustered family and twin structures in the Human Connectome Project when observations violate independence.
  • State of the art — Spisak et al. (2023), Multivariate BWAS can be replicable with moderate sample sizes, Nature. Evaluates sample-size requirements, sampling variability, and univariate versus multivariate power in brain-wide association studies.
  • Critical evaluation — Yarkoni (2009), Big Correlations in Little Studies: Inflated fMRI Correlations Reflect Low Statistical Power—Commentary on Vul et al. (2009), Perspectives on Psychological Science. Demonstrates that small-sample fMRI studies systematically report inflated brain-behavior correlations because only extreme sampling fluctuations cross significance thresholds.

Understand the transformation

1. SOTA & Foundational Paper Motivation: Sampling Variability, Winner's Curse, and Family Clustering in BWAS

Why did decades of small-sample neuroimaging studies (\(N = 20\text{–}50\)) report large brain-behavior correlations (\(|r| = 0.45\text{–}0.75\)) that failed to replicate in large consortia? And why does pooling \(4,000\) scans from twins, siblings, and longitudinal repeats not provide \(4,000\) independent degrees of freedom? The landmark paper assigned to this lesson provides the definitive empirical and theoretical answer:

  • Marek et al. (2022), Reproducible brain-wide association studies require thousands of individuals (PD02, Abstract, Figure 1e–h, Figure 3, and Methods on Sampling Variability): By drawing millions of bootstrap and subsampled cohorts across sample sizes \(N \in [25, 4000]\) from ABCD (\(N \approx 3,928\)) and UK Biobank (\(N \approx 32,572\)), Marek et al. demonstrated that true univariate and multivariate brain-phenotype correlations in population neuroscience are small (\(|r_{\text{true}}| \approx 0.05\text{–}0.15\)). Because the standard error of a sample correlation scales as \(\text{SE}(r) \approx \frac{1 - r^2}{\sqrt{N - 3}} \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{N - 3}}\), at \(N = 25\) the sampling standard deviation is \(\sigma_r \approx 0.21\)—wider than the true effect itself. Consequently, applying a significance filter (\(p < 0.05\), requiring \(|r_{\text{obs}}| > 0.396\) at \(N=25\)) acts as a selection sieve: only samples that suffered massive random sampling inflation (Type M / Magnitude Error, often \(4\times\text{–}7\times\) the true effect) or even opposite-sign fluctuations (Type S / Sign Error, up to \(15\%\text{–}30\%\) of significant hits) pass the threshold. Furthermore, consortia such as ABCD and HCP deliberately oversample monozygotic/dizygotic twins and siblings, introducing positive intracluster correlation (\(\rho > 0\)) that reduces the effective sample size \(N_{\text{eff}} < N_{\text{rows}}\).

2. University Curriculum Alignment

This lesson bridges UC Berkeley Data 8 (Chapter 14.5: Variability of the Sample Mean) and Data 100 (Central Limit Theorem, Fisher's \(z\)-Transformation, and Clustered Survey Sampling) with Graduate Neuroimaging Data Science and Graduate fMRI Analysis (Power, Precision, and Gelman & Carlin's Type S / Type M Errors). We derive the exact variance of clustered observations, Kish's (1965) Design Effect (\(\text{DEFF}\)) and Effective Sample Size (\(N_{\text{eff}}\)), and the sampling distribution of Pearson \(r\) across \(N\).

3. Mathematical Formulation: Clustered Variance, Effective Sample Size, and the BWAS Sampling Funnel

A. Variance of the Sample Mean Under Within-Cluster Dependence

Consider a neuroimaging dataset of \(N_{\text{total}} = C \cdot m\) observations organized into \(C\) independent clusters (e.g., \(C\) families/twins or \(C\) participants) with \(m\) repeated observations per cluster. Let \(Y_{ci} = \mu + u_c + \epsilon_{ci}\), where \(u_c \sim (0, \sigma_b^2)\) is the between-cluster random effect and \(\epsilon_{ci} \sim (0, \sigma_w^2)\) is the within-cluster residual, with total marginal variance \(\sigma^2 = \sigma_b^2 + \sigma_w^2\). The Intracluster Correlation Coefficient (ICC) is:

\[\rho = \text{Corr}(Y_{ci}, Y_{cj}) = \frac{\sigma_b^2}{\sigma_b^2 + \sigma_w^2} \in [0, 1] \qquad (i \neq j)\]

Averaging across all \(C\) independent clusters yields the exact variance of the grand mean \(\bar{Y} = \frac{1}{Cm} \sum_{c=1}^C \sum_{i=1}^m Y_{ci}\):

\[\text{Var}(\bar{Y}) = \frac{\sigma^2}{C m} \left[1 + (m - 1)\rho\right] = \frac{\sigma^2}{N_{\text{eff}}}, \qquad \text{where } N_{\text{eff}} = \frac{C m}{1 + (m - 1)\rho} = \frac{N_{\text{total}}}{\text{DEFF}}\]

  • When \(\rho = 0\) (independent scans): \(\text{DEFF} = 1\) and \(N_{\text{eff}} = C m = N_{\text{total}}\).
  • When \(\rho = 1\) (identical clones/duplicates): \(\text{DEFF} = m\) and \(N_{\text{eff}} = C\) (repeating the scan adds zero new information).
  • Naive \(t\)-tests that divide by \(\sqrt{N_{\text{total}}}\) instead of \(\sqrt{N_{\text{eff}}}\) underestimate the standard error by a factor of \(\sqrt{\text{DEFF}} = \sqrt{1 + (m-1)\rho}\), inflating \(t\)-statistics and driving false-positive rates far above \(\alpha = 0.05\).

B. Fisher's \(z\)-Transform and Type M / Type S Errors in Small-\(N\) BWAS (PD02)

For a bivariate population with true correlation \(r_{\text{true}}\), Fisher's variance-stabilizing transformation is:

\[z = \text{arctanh}(r) = \frac{1}{2} \ln\!\left(\frac{1 + r}{1 - r}\right) \stackrel{\text{approx}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}\!\left(\text{arctanh}(r_{\text{true}}),\; \frac{1}{N_{\text{eff}} - 3}\right)\]

When a study tests \(H_0: r = 0\) at two-sided level \(\alpha = 0.05\), only realizations exceeding the critical threshold \(|r_{\text{crit}}(N)| = \tanh(1.96 / \sqrt{N - 3})\) are published as "statistically significant". Following Gelman & Carlin (2014) and Marek et al. (PD02):

  1. Type M Error (Exaggeration Ratio): \(\mathbb{E}\!\left[|r_{\text{obs}}| / |r_{\text{true}}| \;\big|\; |r_{\text{obs}}| > r_{\text{crit}}(N)\right]\).
  2. Type S Error (Sign Flip Probability): \(\mathbb{P}\!\left(\text{sign}(r_{\text{obs}}) \neq \text{sign}(r_{\text{true}}) \;\big|\; |r_{\text{obs}}| > r_{\text{crit}}(N)\right)\).

Part 1 · Core Mathematical Implementation: Clustered Variance, Kish's \(N_{\text{eff}}\), and Empirical Verification

We simulate \(M = 4,000\) Monte Carlo realizations of a clustered neuroimaging cohort with \(C = 60\) participants (clusters), \(m = 4\) repeated scans per participant (\(N_{\text{total}} = 240\) scan rows), and intracluster correlation \(\rho = 0.80\). We verify that the empirical standard deviation of the grand mean across Monte Carlo trials matches \(\sigma / \sqrt{N_{\text{eff}}}\) to three decimal places, whereas the naive standard error \(s / \sqrt{N_{\text{total}}}\) underestimates true sampling uncertainty by a factor of \(\sqrt{1 + (m-1)\rho} = \sqrt{3.4} \approx 1.844\).

sigma_total = 1.0

n_eff_theo = n_total / deff_theo
se_true_theo = sigma_total / np.sqrt(n_eff_theo)
se_naive_theo = sigma_total / np.sqrt(n_total)

sigma_b = np.sqrt(rho_icc) * sigma_total
sigma_w = np.sqrt(1.0 - rho_icc) * sigma_total

r_true = 0.10
def simulate_bwas_sampling(n_sample: int, r_pop: float, n_draws: int = 15000) -> dict:
    # Exact Fisher z sampling with finite-sample correlation distribution
    z_pop = np.arctanh(r_pop)
    se_z = 1.0 / np.sqrt(n_sample - 3.0)
    r_draws = np.tanh(rng.normal(z_pop, se_z, size=n_draws))
    t_stats = r_draws * np.sqrt((n_sample - 2.0) / np.maximum(1.0 - r_draws**2, 1e-12))
    p_vals = 2.0 * (1.0 - student_t.cdf(np.abs(t_stats), df=n_sample - 2))
    sig_mask = p_vals < 0.05
    r_sig = r_draws[sig_mask]
    power = float(sig_mask.mean())
    type_m = float(np.mean(np.abs(r_sig)) / abs(r_pop)) if len(r_sig) > 0 else np.nan
    type_s = float(np.mean(np.sign(r_sig) != np.sign(r_pop))) if len(r_sig) > 0 else np.nan
    return {
        'N': n_sample,
        'r_draws': r_draws,
        'sig_mask': sig_mask,
        'power_pct': round(100.0 * power, 1),
        'mean_abs_r_if_sig': round(float(np.mean(np.abs(r_sig))), 3),
        'type_M_exaggeration': round(type_m, 2),
        'type_S_sign_error_pct': round(100.0 * type_s, 1),
        'ci_95_width': round(float(np.percentile(r_draws, 97.5) - np.percentile(r_draws, 2.5)), 3),
    }

bwas_25 = simulate_bwas_sampling(25, r_true)

def audit_clustered_cohort(label: str, n_clusters_c: int, m_repeats: int, icc_rho: float) -> dict:
    n_tot = n_clusters_c * m_repeats
    deff = 1.0 + (m_repeats - 1) * icc_rho
    n_eff = n_tot / deff
    # Minimum detectable correlation |r| at 80% power, two-sided alpha=0.05: z_alpha/2 + z_beta = 1.96 + 0.84 = 2.80
    min_r_naive = float(np.tanh(2.80 / np.sqrt(max(n_tot - 3.0, 1.0))))
    min_r_true = float(np.tanh(2.80 / np.sqrt(max(n_eff - 3.0, 1.0))))
    return {
        'Cohort_Design': label,
        'N_Rows': n_tot,
        'Clusters_C': n_clusters_c,
        'ICC_rho': icc_rho,
        'DEFF': round(deff, 2),
        'N_eff': round(n_eff, 1),
        'Naive_MDE_r': round(min_r_naive, 3),
        'True_MDE_r_80pct': round(min_r_true, 3),
    }

  1. Make the specified wrong choice above. Compare its result with the reference checks; explain why the misleading result is possible:

Deliverable focus: Sampling distributions, Kish's clustered Design Effect (\(\text{DEFF} = 1 + (m-1)\rho\)), effective sample size \(N_{\text{eff}}\), and small-\(N\) Type M / Type S errors (PD02)

1. Suggestive Prompt (in the spirit of what to ask — adapt, do not copy verbatim)

Ask Goose (Qwen) to compute Kish's Design Effect (\(\text{DEFF}\)), effective sample size \(N_{\text{eff}}\), and empirical false-positive rate when 240 scans come from C = 60 participants (\(m = 4\) scans/person, \(\text{ICC } \rho = 0.80\)), and simulate Type M (magnitude exaggeration) and Type S (sign) errors for a true BWAS correlation \(r = 0.10\) across \(N \in [25, 2000]\). Ask Gemma 4 why treating 240 clustered scans as \(df = 239\) inflates false positives 6-fold.

2. What to Look For & How to Trace the Error Back Down

  • Immediate Red Flag: A study with C = 60 participants and m = 4 repeated scans runs a 240-row \(t\)-test (df = 239) and suffers a 29.3% false-positive rate at nominal \(\alpha = 0.05\), or an underpowered \(N = 25\) BWAS study reports a 'significant' \(|\hat{r}| = 0.47\) when the true population correlation is \(r = 0.10\).
  • Where the Bug Entered: Ignoring within-participant intraclass correlation \(\rho = 0.80\) underestimates the variance of the mean by \(\text{DEFF} = 1 + (4-1)(0.80) = 3.40\times\), and conditioning on \(p < 0.05\) in an underpowered sample (\(7.7\%\) power at \(N = 25\)) selects only extreme noise tails (the winner's curse).
  • How to Fix It: Aggregate repeated measures to the participant level (df = C - 1 = 59) or fit a mixed model, and size BWAS cohorts using \(N_{\text{eff}} = N_{\text{total}} / \text{DEFF}\).

3. Expected Outcome Range & Why It Must Look That Way

Expected Range: In DS07, \(\text{DEFF} = 3.40\) reduces 240 scan rows to N_eff = 70.6 effective observations; aggregating to C = 60 subjects restores the empirical false-positive rate from 29.3% down to 5.2%. For a true BWAS effect \(r = 0.10\), significant hits at \(N = 25\) (7.7% power) suffer a 4.71x Type M inflation (mean significant \(|\hat{r}| = 0.471\)) and 9.7% Type S sign error, converging toward 1.0x only as \(N \ge 1{,}000\) (PD02). Why: Only realizations with \(|\hat{r}| > r_{\text{crit}}(N) \approx 2/\sqrt{N}\) pass significance; when \(r_{\text{crit}} \gg r_{\text{true}}\), every 'discovery' is dominated by sampling noise.
