Quantifies how much predictor j's variance is inflated by correlation with other predictors (VIF) and how strongly observation i pulls the fitted parameter vector (Cook's D_i).

Research question

Reading: PD02, PD07. Review the assigned figure or result before starting the lesson.

Question: Could correlated predictors or influential participants change a coefficient's apparent meaning?

Record a prediction, a source location, and one point you want this lesson to clarify. Ask your AI tutor to distinguish the paper’s evidence from its interpretation.

Why this technique matters

Brain-behavior and morphometric regressions frequently combine modest sample sizes with heavy-tailed motion or segmentation outliers and highly correlated bilateral or multi-site predictors. A single high-leverage outlier can invert an ordinary least-squares slope and manufacture a false p < 0.01 discovery (Cook, 1977; Rousselet & Pernet, 2012), while high predictor collinearity (large VIF) inflates standard errors and causes erratic sign flips across homologous regions. Auditing leverage h_ii, Cook's distance D_i, VIF, and residual structure before reporting p-values prevents fragile or misspecified claims.

Key references · Regression diagnostics: hat-matrix leverage, studentized residuals, Cook's distance, Sherman-Morrison LOO deletion, and Variance Inflation Factors (VIF)

  • Foundational — Cook (1977), Detection of Influential Observation in Linear Regression, Technometrics. Derives Cook's distance D_i combining studentized residuals and hat-matrix leverage h_ii to quantify how deleting a single observation shifts the fitted regression vector.
  • State of the art — Kang et al. (2024), Study design features increase replicability in brain-wide association studies, Nature. Demonstrates how predictor variance spread and study design features govern effect-size stability and replicability in brain-wide association regressions. Course library: PD06.
  • Critical evaluation — Rousselet and Pernet (2012), Improving standards in brain-behavior correlation analyses, Frontiers in Human Neuroscience. Shows how high-leverage outliers distort ordinary least-squares and Pearson brain-behavior correlations in small neuroimaging samples and advocates robust diagnostics.
  • Review — Wilcox and Rousselet (2023), An Updated Guide to Robust Statistical Methods in Neuroscience, Current Protocols. Provides an updated methodological guide to outlier detection, leverage diagnostics, and robust statistical estimation in neuroscience.

Understand the transformation

1. Motivation from the Assigned Papers: Why Single High-Leverage Scans and Collinear Covariates Flip Brain-Behavior Slopes

Fitting a linear model and reporting a \(p\)-value without inspecting regression diagnostics—hat-matrix leverage \(h_{ii}\), Studentized residuals \(t_i\), Cook's distance \(D_i\), and Variance Inflation Factors (\(\text{VIF}_j\))—is one of the most frequent sources of irreproducible claims in human neuroimaging:

  • Marek et al. (2022), Reproducible brain-wide association studies require thousands of individuals (PD02, Figure 1, Figure 3, and Methods on Outlier Leverage & Sample Instability): In small-to-moderate neuroimaging cohorts (\(N = 30\text{–}150\)), a single participant who lies at the extreme tail of both predictor space (e.g., an unflagged segmentation error or extreme head-size / age value, giving high leverage \(h_{ii}\)) and outcome space (giving a large residual \(e_i\)) exerts massive Cook's distance \(D_i\) on the fitted slope. Removing that single participant can flip a "statistically significant" positive brain-behavior slope (\(\hat{\beta} > 0, p < 0.01\)) into a null or negative slope.
  • Fortin et al. (2018), Harmonization of cortical thickness measurements across scanners and sites (PD07, Introduction, Methods Section 2.3, and Discussion on Confounding Between Site and Biological Group): When multi-site studies regress cortical thickness onto biological covariates (Age, Intracranial Volume, Clinical Diagnosis) alongside correlated scanner/site indicators or redundant anatomical measures (e.g., entering Left Hemisphere Thickness, Right Hemisphere Thickness, and Whole-Brain Mean Thickness simultaneously), severe multicollinearity (\(\text{VIF}_j \gg 10\)) inflates the standard errors of individual partial regression coefficients \(\hat{\beta}_j\) by \(\sqrt{\text{VIF}_j}\), causing individual slopes to oscillate wildly across sites even when the overall model prediction \(\hat{y}\) is accurate.

2. Relation to Graduate Courses and Upstream Modules

This lesson synthesizes UC Berkeley Data 8 (Chapter 15.5: Visual Diagnostics) and Data 100 (GLM Residual Diagnostics, Hat Matrix Leverage, Cook's Distance, and Multicollinearity / VIF) with USC NIIN 540/580 and Stanford Psych 204B. We derive the exact algebraic relationship between the projection matrix diagonal \(h_{ii} = P_{ii}\), leave-one-out parameter deletion \(\hat{\beta} - \hat{\beta}_{(-i)}\), Cook's distance \(D_i\), and the Variance Inflation Factor \(\text{VIF}_j = (1 - R_j^2)^{-1}\).

3. Mathematical Formulation: Leverage \(h_{ii}\), Leave-One-Out Deletion, Cook's Distance \(D_i\), and \(\text{VIF}_j\)

Consider the linear model \(y = X\beta + \epsilon\) with full-rank design matrix \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times p}\) (\(N > p\)), OLS estimator \(\hat{\beta} = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y\), fitted vector \(\hat{y} = P y\), and residual vector \(e = (I_N - P)y\), where \(P = X(X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top\) is the hat matrix (orthogonal projector onto \(\mathcal{C}(X)\)).

A. Hat-Matrix Leverage \(h_{ii}\) and Residual Variance Shrinkage

The \(i\)-th diagonal entry of \(P\), denoted \(h_{ii} = P_{ii} = x_i^\top (X^\top X)^{-1} x_i\), measures the leverage (Mahalanobis distance from the predictor centroid) of observation \(i\):

\[\frac{1}{N} \le h_{ii} \le 1, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{N} h_{ii} = \text{tr}(P) = p, \qquad \bar{h} = \frac{p}{N}\]

Because \(\hat{y}_i = \sum_{j=1}^N P_{ij} y_j = h_{ii} y_i + \sum_{j \neq i} P_{ij} y_j\), we have \(\frac{\partial \hat{y}_i}{\partial y_i} = h_{ii}\). As \(h_{ii} \to 1\), the regression surface is forced to pass directly through \((x_i, y_i)\), shrinking its raw residual variance:

\[\text{Var}(e_i) = \sigma^2 (1 - h_{ii}) \implies r_i^{\text{std}} = \frac{e_i}{\hat{\sigma}\sqrt{1 - h_{ii}}}\]

Thus, a high-leverage point (\(h_{ii} > \frac{2p}{N}\) or \(\frac{3p}{N}\)) can have a deceptively small raw residual \(e_i\) simply because it pulled the regression line toward itself.

B. Exact Leave-One-Out Deletion Identity and Cook's Distance \(D_i\)

By the Sherman-Morrison rank-1 update formula applied to \((X_{(-i)}^\top X_{(-i)})^{-1} = (X^\top X - x_i x_i^\top)^{-1}\), removing participant \(i\) shifts the OLS coefficient vector by:

\[\hat{\beta} - \hat{\beta}_{(-i)} = \frac{(X^\top X)^{-1} x_i \, e_i}{1 - h_{ii}}\]

Cook's Distance \(D_i\) measures the squared Mahalanobis distance between \(\hat{\beta}\) and the leave-one-out estimate \(\hat{\beta}_{(-i)}\) (equivalently, the standardized change in all \(N\) fitted values \(\|\hat{y} - \hat{y}_{(-i)}\|_2^2 / (p\hat{\sigma}^2)\)):

\[D_i = \frac{(\hat{\beta} - \hat{\beta}_{(-i)})^\top (X^\top X) (\hat{\beta} - \hat{\beta}_{(-i)})}{p\,\hat{\sigma}^2} = \frac{e_i^2}{p\,\hat{\sigma}^2} \cdot \frac{h_{ii}}{(1 - h_{ii})^2} = \frac{(r_i^{\text{std}})^2}{p} \cdot \frac{h_{ii}}{1 - h_{ii}}\]

Notice that \(D_i\) is the product of standardized residual squared and the leverage odds ratio \(\frac{h_{ii}}{1 - h_{ii}}\): an observation is catastrophic (\(D_i > 4/N\) or \(D_i > 0.5\)) when it combines both unusual predictor coordinates (\(h_{ii}\) large) and discrepancy from the trend (\(r_i^{\text{std}}\) large).

C. Variance Inflation Factor (\(\text{VIF}_j\)) Under Predictor Multicollinearity

For standardized predictors \(X_1, \dots, X_{p-1}\), the sampling variance of the \(j\)-th partial slope \(\hat{\beta}_j\) is:

\[\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_j) = \frac{\sigma^2}{(N-1) s_{X_j}^2} \cdot \text{VIF}_j, \qquad \text{where } \boxed{\text{VIF}_j = \frac{1}{1 - R_j^2} = \left[R_{XX}^{-1}\right]_{jj}}\]

When two cortical morphometry predictors correlate at \(r = 0.98\) (\(R_j^2 = 0.9604\)), \(\text{VIF}_j = 25.25\), inflating standard errors by \(\sqrt{25.25} \approx 5.02\times\).

Part 1 · Core Mathematical Implementation: Exact Hat-Matrix Leverage \(h_{ii}\), Studentized Residuals, and Cook's Distance \(D_i\)

We construct a neuroimaging cohort of \(N = 50\) participants (\(p = 2\): intercept + prefrontal cortical thickness predictor). In the clean core of 49 participants, the true association with behavioral score \(y\) is slightly negative (\(\beta_1 = -0.20\)). Participant 50 is a high-leverage segmentation artifact (\(x_{50} = +4.6\text{ SD}, y_{50} = +4.4\text{ SD}\)). We implement the exact hat matrix \(P\), verify \(\sum h_{ii} = p = 2\), verify the Sherman-Morrison leave-one-out deletion identity \(\hat{\beta} - \hat{\beta}_{(-i)} = \frac{(X^\top X)^{-1} x_i e_i}{1 - h_{ii}}\) to machine precision (\(10^{-14}\)), and compute Cook's distance \(D_i\).

Python · Executable Cell 1
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy.stats import t as student_t

rng = np.random.default_rng(1313)
N_subj = 50
x_core = rng.normal(0.0, 0.85, size=N_subj - 1)
y_core = 10.0 - 0.28 * x_core + rng.normal(0.0, 0.70, size=N_subj - 1)

# Append 1 high-leverage segmentation artifact participant (index 49)
x_all = np.append(x_core, 4.60)
y_all = np.append(y_core, 14.50)
X_design = np.column_stack([np.ones(N_subj), x_all])
p_cols = X_design.shape[1]

# Fit OLS on all 50 subjects vs clean 49 subjects
XtX_inv = np.linalg.inv(X_design.T @ X_design)
beta_all = XtX_inv @ (X_design.T @ y_all)
beta_clean49 = np.linalg.lstsq(X_design[:-1], y_all[:-1], rcond=None)[0]

# Hat matrix P = X (X^T X)^-1 X^T and leverage h_ii = diag(P)
P_hat = X_design @ XtX_inv @ X_design.T
h_lev = np.diag(P_hat)
e_raw = y_all - X_design @ beta_all
sigma2_hat = float(np.sum(e_raw ** 2) / (N_subj - p_cols))

# Standardized residuals and exact Cook's distance D_i
r_std = e_raw / np.sqrt(sigma2_hat * (1.0 - h_lev))
cooks_D = (r_std ** 2 / p_cols) * (h_lev / (1.0 - h_lev))

# Verify Sherman-Morrison Leave-One-Out (LOO) identity for participant 50 (index -1)
loo_shift_formula = (XtX_inv @ X_design[-1] * e_raw[-1]) / (1.0 - h_lev[-1])
beta_loo_exact = beta_all - loo_shift_formula

assert np.isclose(np.sum(h_lev), p_cols, atol=1e-12)
assert np.allclose(beta_loo_exact, beta_clean49, atol=1e-12)

# Compute p-values for slope beta_1 with vs without the single high-leverage outlier
se_b1_all = float(np.sqrt(sigma2_hat * XtX_inv[1, 1]))
t_b1_all = float(beta_all[1] / se_b1_all)
p_b1_all = float(2.0 * (1.0 - student_t.cdf(abs(t_b1_all), df=N_subj - 2)))

e_49 = y_all[:-1] - X_design[:-1] @ beta_clean49
s2_49 = float(np.sum(e_49**2) / (N_subj - 3))
se_b1_49 = float(np.sqrt(s2_49 * np.linalg.inv(X_design[:-1].T @ X_design[:-1])[1, 1]))
t_b1_49 = float(beta_clean49[1] / se_b1_49)
p_b1_49 = float(2.0 * (1.0 - student_t.cdf(abs(t_b1_49), df=N_subj - 3)))

print(f"Sum of leverages tr(P): {np.sum(h_lev):.4f} (equals p = {p_cols})")
print(f"Outlier Subject 50 -> Leverage h_50 = {h_lev[-1]:.3f} ({h_lev[-1]/(p_cols/N_subj):.1f}x average!), Cook's D_50 = {cooks_D[-1]:.3f}")
print(f"Slope WITH Subject 50 (N=50):    beta_1 = {beta_all[1]:+.3f} (t = {t_b1_all:+.2f}, p = {p_b1_all:.4f} -> False 'Significant' Positive!)")
print(f"Slope WITHOUT Subject 50 (N=49): beta_1 = {beta_clean49[1]:+.3f} (t = {t_b1_49:+.2f}, p = {p_b1_49:.4f} -> True Negative Trend!)")
print(f"Sherman-Morrison LOO identity error: {np.max(np.abs(beta_loo_exact - beta_clean49)):.2e}")

x_out_grid = np.linspace(0.0, 5.0, 20)
leverage_sweep = []

2. Breaking the Contract: Diagnosis of High-Leverage Slope Reversal & High \(\text{VIF}\)

  1. Make the specified wrong choice above. Compare its result with the reference checks; explain why the misleading result is possible:
    • Why did a single participant (Subj 50) flip the cohort slope from \(-0.16\) to \(+0.36\) (\(p = 0.002\)), and why doesn't a vertical outlier at \(x = \bar{x}\) do the same? Because the leverage of observation \(i\) on the slope is proportional to \((x_i - \bar{x})^2\). At \(x_i = \bar{x}\), \(h_{ii} = 1/N\) and the observation pulls the intercept \(\hat{\beta}_0\) vertically without rotating the slope \(\hat{\beta}_1\). At \(x_{50} = +4.6\text{ SD}\), \(h_{50,50} = 0.32\) (\(8\times\) the average leverage), acting as a long mechanical lever arm that rotates the entire regression line.
    • Why did entering Left_ROI and Right_ROI simultaneously (r = 0.97, \(\text{VIF} = 19\)) produce opposite-sign weights in \(>40\%\) of fits? Because when \(\text{VIF}_j = (1 - R_j^2)^{-1}\) is large, the sampling covariance between \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{Left}}\) and \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{Right}}\) is strongly negative (\(\text{Corr}(\hat{\beta}_1, \hat{\beta}_2) = -r_{12} = -0.97\)). Small noise fluctuations add \(+c\) to one hemisphere and \(-c\) to the other while keeping their sum \((\hat{\beta}_1 + \hat{\beta}_2)\) nearly constant.

2. What to Look For & How to Trace the Error Back Down

  • Immediate Red Flag: Full-sample OLS reports a 'statistically significant' positive brain-behavior slope (\(\hat{\beta}_1 = +0.358, t = +2.83, p = 0.0067\)), yet deleting a single participant (Subject 50) flips the slope to negative (-0.10, non-significant); or entering correlated Left and Right ROI volumes (\(r = 0.9736\)) gives one hemisphere a strong positive weight and the other a strong negative weight.
  • Where the Bug Entered: Subject 50 sits at extreme predictor leverage (\(h_{50} = 0.484\), 12.1x the average leverage \(\bar{h} = p/N = 0.04\), Cook's \(D_{50} = 8.761 \gg 1\)), and bilateral ROI collinearity inflates \(\text{VIF} = 1 / (1 - 0.9736^2) = 19.55\), making \(\text{Cov}(\hat{\beta}_L, \hat{\beta}_R)\) strongly negative.
  • How to Fix It: Flag any observation with \(h_{ii} > 2p/N\) or Cook's \(D_i > 4/N\) (or \(D_i > 1\)), verify LOO slope stability via Sherman-Morrison \(\hat{\beta}_{(-i)} = \hat{\beta} - (X^\top X)^{-1} x_i e_i / (1 - h_{ii})\), and combine or ridge-regularize collinear bilateral ROIs (VIF > 5–10).

3. Expected Outcome Range & Why It Must Look That Way

Expected Range: In DS13, leverages sum to tr(P) = 2.0000; Subject 50 has h_50 = 0.484 and Cook's D = 8.761, driving the entire apparent effect (+0.358, p = 0.0067 with Subject 50 vs. -0.10 without Subject 50); on collinear bilateral ROIs (r = 0.9736), VIF = 19.55 causes OLS weights on true (+0.50, +0.50) predictors to flip to opposite signs in a large fraction of cohorts. Why: Cook's distance scales as \(D_i = \frac{r_i^2}{p} \frac{h_{ii}}{1 - h_{ii}}\), which explodes as \(h_{ii} \to 1\).
