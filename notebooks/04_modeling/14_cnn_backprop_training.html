Why this technique matters

Convolutional layers underpin 3D structural and functional MRI encoders (ResNets, U-Nets, BrainIAC, OpenMind) by sharing local kernel weights across space rather than fitting >10^8 dense parameters. However, hand-derived or custom backpropagation kernels can fail silently without centered finite-difference verification, and Empirical Risk Minimization will readily latch onto hospital-specific acquisition tags, field-of-view cropping, or corner calibration markers (shortcut learning; Zech et al., 2018; Geirhos et al., 2020), achieving near-100% internal accuracy while collapsing to chance on clean external scans.

Key references · Convolutional neural network (CNN) weight sharing, analytical backpropagation, finite-difference gradient checking, and shortcut-learning audits

  • Foundational — Rumelhart et al. (1986), Learning representations by back-propagating errors, Nature. Establishes reverse-mode chain-rule backpropagation for training multi-layer neural network representations.
  • Foundational — LeCun et al. (1998), Gradient-based learning applied to document recognition, Proceedings of the IEEE. Introduces convolutional weight sharing and translation-equivariant feature maps optimized end-to-end by gradient descent.
  • Foundational — He et al. (2016), Deep Residual Learning for Image Recognition, 2016 IEEE Conference on Computer Vision and Pattern Recognition (CVPR). Introduces residual skip connections (ResNet), the backbone architecture used in BrainIAC (PM03) and OpenMind (PM04) 3D medical CNNs.
  • Critical evaluation — Geirhos et al. (2020), Shortcut learning in deep neural networks, Nature Machine Intelligence. Explains why gradient-trained deep networks preferentially exploit simple spurious covariates (such as scanner tags) unless audited on unconfounded out-of-distribution data.

1. Foundational & SOTA Paper Motivation: Inside Convolutional Backpropagation & Shortcut Learning

Deep 3D Convolutional Neural Networks (ResNets, He et al., 2016; ConvNeXts; U-Nets) form core backbones in modern structural and functional MRI foundation models, including BrainIAC (Tak et al., 2026; PM03) and the OpenMind 3D SSL Benchmark (Wald et al., 2025; PM04). Building and auditing a convolutional network from scratch (Rumelhart, Hinton, & Williams, 1986, Nature; LeCun et al., 1998, Proc. IEEE; Neuromatch Academy Deep Learning) makes three engineering and scientific principles explicit:

  1. Weight Sharing & Translation Equivariance (LeCun et al., 1998): A fully connected layer mapping a \(64 \times 64 \times 64\) 3D MRI volume (\(262{,}144\) voxels) to \(512\) hidden units requires \(1.34 \times 10^8\) parameters—creating severe overfitting risk on typical clinical cohorts. A convolutional layer slides a small local kernel \(K \in \mathbb{R}^{F \times k_x \times k_y}\) (or \(F \times k_x \times k_y \times k_z\) in 3D MRI) across all spatial locations, sharing the same \(F \cdot k_x \cdot k_y\) weights everywhere and encoding a translation-equivariant inductive bias for local spatial motifs.
  2. Analytical Backpropagation vs. Finite-Difference Verification (Rumelhart et al., 1986): Deep learning training loops rely on the multivariate chain rule to propagate scalar loss gradients \(\partial \mathcal{L} / \partial z\) backward through linear readouts, elementwise nonlinearities (tanh or ReLU), and spatial convolution tensors (einsum). A transpose, sign, or reduction-axis bug can silently corrupt optimization. A standard numerical check is centered finite-difference gradient verification: comparing the analytical partial derivative \(\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial K_{f,i,j}}\) against \(\frac{\mathcal{L}(K + \epsilon e_{f,i,j}) - \mathcal{L}(K - \epsilon e_{f,i,j})}{2\epsilon}\) (achieving \(< 10^{-7}\) tolerance, and \(\approx 5.5 \times 10^{-11}\) in IEEE-754 float64 here).
  3. Shortcut Learning & Confound Exploitation (Zech et al., 2018, PLOS Med.; Geirhos et al., 2020, Nat. Mach. Intell.; DeGrave et al., 2021, Nat. Mach. Intell.): Gradient descent minimizes training cross-entropy along whichever features provide the steepest loss reduction. As shown in clinical imaging audits where CNNs latched onto hospital-specific corner markers or field-of-view cropping rather than pathology (Zech et al., 2018; DeGrave et al., 2021), a confounded training set allows a CNN to achieve near-perfect accuracy on scans containing the tag while failing on clean external scans.

2. Mathematical Formulation & Exact Chain-Rule Derivation

2.1 Forward Pass: Patch Extraction, Convolution, Nonlinearity & Logistic Readout

Let \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times H \times W}\) be a batch of \(N\) 2D images (\(H = W = 9\)). Using sliding_window_view, we extract all valid \(k \times k\) (\(k = 3\)) spatial patches into a 5D tensor \(P \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times H' \times W' \times k \times k}\) where \(H' = W' = H - k + 1 = 7\) and \(P_{n, x, y, i, j} = X_{n, x+i, y+j}\). Given \(F = 2\) convolutional filters \(K \in \mathbb{R}^{F \times k \times k}\), linear readout weights \(v \in \mathbb{R}^{H' W' F}\), and scalar bias \(b \in \mathbb{R}\):

  1. Spatial Convolution & Hyperbolic Tangent Activation:

\[u_{n, x, y, f} = \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} \sum_{j=0}^{k-1} P_{n, x, y, i, j} K_{f, i, j}, \qquad h_{n, x, y, f} = \tanh\left( u_{n, x, y, f} \right) \in (-1, 1)\]

2.2 Backward Pass: Analytical Gradients via the Multivariate Chain Rule

Differentiating \(\mathcal{L}\) step-by-step from the scalar output back to the convolutional kernel \(K\):

  1. Logit Gradient \(\delta z \in \mathbb{R}^N\):

\[\delta z_n = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z_n} = \frac{1}{N} \left( \hat{p}_n - y_n \right)\]

import numpy as np
from numpy.lib.stride_tricks import sliding_window_view
from scipy.special import expit

rng = np.random.default_rng(414)
n = 240
y = rng.integers(0, 2, n)
images = rng.normal(0, 0.18, (n, 9, 9))
for i, label in enumerate(y):
    pos = rng.integers(2, 7)
    if label:
        images[i, 2:7, pos] += 1.0
    else:
        images[i, pos, 2:7] += 1.0

patches = sliding_window_view(images, (3, 3), axis=(1, 2))
tr, te = np.arange(160), np.arange(160, 240)
K = rng.normal(0, 0.25, (2, 3, 3))
v = rng.normal(0, 0.15, 7 * 7 * 2)
b = 0.0


def forward_grad(P, target, K, v, b):
    conv = np.einsum("nxyij,fij->nxyf", P, K)
    h = np.tanh(conv)
    flat = h.reshape(len(P), -1)
    logit = flat @ v + b
    loss = np.mean(np.logaddexp(0, logit) - target * logit)
    dz = (expit(logit) - target) / len(P)
    gv = flat.T @ dz
    gb = dz.sum()
    dh = (dz[:, None] * v[None, :]).reshape(h.shape) * (1 - h * h)
    gK = np.einsum("nxyij,nxyf->fij", P, dh)
    return loss, (gK, gv, gb), expit(logit)


loss, grad, _ = forward_grad(patches[tr], y[tr], K, v, b)
eps = 1e-5
plus = K.copy()
minus = K.copy()
plus[0, 1, 1] += eps
minus[0, 1, 1] -= eps
numeric = (forward_grad(patches[tr], y[tr], plus, v, b)[0] - forward_grad(patches[tr], y[tr], minus, v, b)[0]) / (2 * eps)
print("Analytic / finite-difference kernel gradient:", grad[0][0, 1, 1], numeric)
assert np.isclose(numeric, grad[0][0, 1, 1], atol=1e-7)

Predict the behavior before running the next cell:

Exit questions and answer key

  1. Why do we verify analytical backpropagation gradients against centered finite differences \(\frac{\mathcal{L}(\theta+\epsilon) - \mathcal{L}(\theta-\epsilon)}{2\epsilon}\) before training? Because centered finite differences approximate the true derivative to \(O(\epsilon^2)\) directly from the forward loss function; agreement to \(<10^{-7}\) proves our analytical backward chain rule (dh, gK, gv, gb) is free of calculus or indexing bugs.

  2. Why is finite-difference gradient evaluation only used for debugging and not for training a 10-million-parameter 3D MRI network? Finite differences require \(2 P\) separate forward passes per step (\(2 \times 10^7\) forward passes for \(10^7\) parameters), whereas analytical backpropagation computes all \(P\) partial derivatives simultaneously in a single forward-backward pass.

  3. Why does weight sharing in a convolutional kernel \(K \in \mathbb{R}^{F \times 3 \times 3}\) help when classifying bars that appear at random spatial positions? The same \(3 \times 3\) kernel slides across all \((x, y)\) patches, so an orientation feature learned at row 2 automatically detects the same bar at row 6 while using only \(18\) kernel parameters.

  4. How did the corner artifact in Panel (C) fool the CNN, and how does this relate to multi-hospital MRI validation (PM03)? Gradient descent follows the steepest reduction in training loss; a spatially fixed, high-contrast scanner artifact has a larger gradient than translation-varying anatomy, causing the network to fail on external hospitals lacking that artifact.
